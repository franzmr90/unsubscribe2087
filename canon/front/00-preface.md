# Preface

I should tell you how this book was made, because I think it matters, and because if I don't say it plainly somebody will eventually work it out and the fact that I didn't say it will become the story.

I wrote this with an AI.

Not "with the help of." Not "assisted by." I sat with a machine for a long time and told it everything I had — every idea, every half-formed connection, every thing I'd been carrying around for years and could never get out of my head in an order that made sense to anyone — and it gave me back a novel that is more mine than anything I have ever managed on my own.

I don't entirely know how to feel about that. I'm going to try to be honest about it rather than resolve it, because I haven't resolved it.

---

Here is what I can tell you about myself that's relevant.

I'm neurodivergent. I see big pictures. I see complex systems and I understand how they fit together, sometimes before I can explain why. Math is intuitive to me in a way that has never required much effort. I have studied a lot of things very deeply, mostly because I couldn't stop, not because anybody asked me to.

And when I try to convey any of it to another person it comes out scattered. It comes out chaotic. I can see the whole shape and I can only ever hand you one corner of it at a time, and by the time I've handed you the third corner you've stopped listening, and I don't blame you, because I've watched myself do it and I wouldn't have listened either.

I've always been ambitious. My ability to execute has never matched it. That gap is most of my adult life.

The first time I used one of these tools properly, I had a week I can only describe as manic. I built a piece of software that solved about eighty percent of the problems I deal with every day running a small business. In seven days. Things I had wanted for years and had never been able to start, because starting them meant holding forty things in my head at once and I could hold maybe six.

What moved that week wasn't my ability. It was the horizon. The line between *things I can imagine* and *things I can actually do* jumped about a mile to the left, and it has not moved back.

This book is on the other side of that line. It was always on the other side of that line. I have had it in my head for over ten years.

---

There's something else, and it took me a while to find the words for it.

I grew up right before computers were everywhere, with a mother who was a programmer, which meant I had one long before most people my age did. And the internet I grew up on was decentralized and weird. It was a mess. You went looking for things and you found strange corners made by strange people who weren't trying to sell you anything, and nobody was watching, and nobody was measuring.

Then, slowly, it centralized. You started getting followed between websites. What you did became a product. And then the algorithms arrived and everything got optimized to hold your attention, and it worked, because it was designed by people much smarter than me to work.

I have watched my own attention span get shorter my whole adult life. It's harder for me to hold something large in my head than it used to be. Harder to sit with a big idea long enough for it to finish arriving.

And the thing I find most thrilling about writing this book — genuinely, the part that lit me up — is that this machine can hold the whole thing at once. It can keep three hundred pages and eighty characters and twenty years of plot in front of it and never lose the thread. It extended my working memory past what a human being gets. It felt like an augmentation. Like a part of my brain that was always supposed to be there finally showing up.

And that scares me, because the same technology that took my attention span is the technology that just gave me my book.

I don't know what to do with that. I was in the driver's seat the whole way. Every idea in this novel is mine. The Bitcoin thread, the surveillance program, what happens to Jerry, the ending — all mine, most of them years old. I chose all of it and I argued about all of it and I threw things out.

But I didn't write it. Not the sentences. And I can't stand here and say I did.

At the same time — I did. Or at least, this is what was in my head, and it has never once come out this way before, and it never would have.

*Am I accelerating the thing this book is afraid of, or am I escaping it using its own tools?*

I genuinely don't know. If I knew, this would be a much less interesting book, and I'd probably have written it a decade ago and it would have been worse.

---

One more thing, about the original.

There is a version of this manuscript from years ago. Twelve thousand words, twenty-three chapters, and it took me a long time to write and I was proud of it. When I started this, my plan was to keep it — to print it in the back, unchanged, so you could see where it came from.

At first, I wanted to preserve my original draft within the manuscript. *It is history*, I told myself. *It is what happened.* But then I realized, history isn't what happened. It's our perception of it. It's the way it's been fed to us. It's the way that we have made the assumptions we needed to in order to swallow the weight of it. And if I left the original manuscript the same, then the continuity of the reality of the present, and the new judgments I have made about the future, would be based on false assumptions. Because the past does change. It changes as our understanding of it does, and most people are doing everything they can to keep the past from changing. Because that is the only way they know how to deal with the fact that change is the only constant.

So it isn't in the back. It's in the front, revised, load-bearing, where it always belonged.

If you want to know what's real in this book and what I made up, there's an appendix. I'd start with the novel.

**Micah Franz**
