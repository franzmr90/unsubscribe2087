# Prologue

**HUT 8, BLETCHLEY PARK**
**BUCKINGHAMSHIRE, ENGLAND**
**11 SEPTEMBER 1941 — 02:14**

The break came at eight minutes past two in the morning, and for about ninety seconds it was the best night of Joan Clarke's life.

She came through from the machine room with the sheets still warm and her hands not quite steady, and she said, "It's out," and four people who had been awake for nineteen hours made a sound that was not quite a cheer, because you learned very quickly in that hut not to make noise about anything.

Then somebody turned the lamp so it fell on the paper, and they read it, and the sound stopped.

*Grid AK 63. Grid AK 66. Grid AL 41.*

Three positions. Three boats. And a fourth signal underneath, a homing order, which meant there was a pack forming and the pack had been told where to form, and any man in that room could take a pencil and put those three coordinates on a chart and draw the shape they made.

It was a net. It was a hundred and ten miles wide and it was lying across the North Channel approaches, and it had been put there for a reason, and the reason was forty-one merchant ships out of Halifax that would sail into the middle of it a little after four o'clock the following afternoon.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere behind the wall the bombes went on turning over, chattering like a room full of knitting needles.

"How long?" said Turing.

He was sitting on the floor with his back against the radiator, which is where he sat, and there was a tin mug chained to the pipe beside his head because somebody had once taken his mug and he had solved the problem the way he solved things. His collar was open. He had a stammer that came and went with how tired he was and it was very bad at two in the morning.

"Fourteen hours," said Clarke. "Give or take."

"And we have their p-positions to within — what."

"Twenty miles. Better, on the AL boat."

"Yes," said Turing. "That's what I th-thought."

He got up off the floor and went and looked at the chart, and he stood there for a long time with his arms folded, and nobody interrupted him, because they had all been in that hut long enough to know what he was doing and roughly how long it took.

He was not looking at the ships.

He was counting the ways the Germans could find out that he had read their mail.

---

That was the whole of it, and everybody in Hut 8 understood it, and not one of them had ever got used to it.

They could read the German navy. Not perfectly and not always and not without the machines and a great deal of luck and the occasional violent theft of a code book off a sinking trawler — but on a good night, on a night like this one, they could sit in a wooden building in Buckinghamshire and know where the U-boats were.

And the moment they used it, they gave it away.

Because a convoy does not turn ninety degrees in open ocean at three in the morning for no reason. It does not thread a gap it has no way of knowing exists. Do that twice and there is a man in Kiel who is not stupid, and who will begin to wonder, and who will change the settings or add a rotor or issue new books to the fleet, and then the machines in the next room stop meaning anything at all, and everything goes black for four months, or six, or forever.

So there was a rule, and the rule was that Bletchley never acted on what Bletchley knew.

Somebody else had to find it. A patrol aircraft had to be flown across the right patch of empty ocean at the right hour so it could send a signal saying it had seen something, and only then could the Admiralty move, and the Germans could shrug and say *they got lucky, they had aircraft up.*

The cover story was not a courtesy. The cover story was the price of the next year of the war.

And in September, in that part of the Atlantic, at that hour, there was no aircraft on earth with the range to be there.

---

Turing did the arithmetic standing up.

He did it the way he did everything, which was completely, without hurrying, and out loud in fragments that nobody else could follow.

Forty-one ships. Call it four hundred souls in the water if the pack got in among them properly, and it would; that was what a pack was for. Say two hundred lost. That was the number on one side.

On the other side was a quantity nobody could observe, which is the hardest kind to weigh and the only kind that mattered. If they moved the convoy and the Germans changed the machine, Bletchley went dark. Not for this convoy — for every convoy. For the tankers and the wheat and the men and the eleven months of the Atlantic that were coming whether anybody liked it or not.

He had made an estimate of that number in July. He had refused to write it down.

The arithmetic came out the same way it had come out in July, and the same way it came out every time anybody in that hut ran it, in any month, with any assumptions, from any direction: it was not close.

It was not close, and it was never close, and that was the thing he could not get past — not that it was hard, but that it was *easy.* It resolved instantly. It resolved every time. A schoolboy could do it. You put two hundred men on one side and eleven thousand on the other and the answer falls out of the page at you, and there is no version of the calculation in which it comes out differently, and no amount of running it again makes it any quieter.

He ran it again anyway.

"Alan," said Clarke, after a while.

"Yes."

"There's nothing."

"No," he said. "There isn't."

He sat back down on the floor with his back against the radiator.

---

Five years earlier, in a paper he had written at twenty-three, Turing had proved a thing that a great many mathematicians had spent a decade hoping was not true — that there is no general procedure by which a machine can determine whether an arbitrary computation will ever come to a stop. Some questions have no answer that any mechanical process can reach, not because the questions are unclear, but because of what a mechanical process *is*.

He thought about that quite often in the hut, usually at about this hour.

Because this problem was not one of those. That was the horror of it. This one had an answer. It was decidable, and computable, and correct, and any machine you could build would arrive at it faster than he had and would not have needed the fourteen hours to be sure.

What was undecidable was whether a man ought to be the sort of thing that acted on it.

There was no procedure for that. He had looked. There was no procedure for that in 1936 and there was none in Hut 8 in 1941 and he had begun to suspect, with the specific dread of somebody who is very good at exactly one thing, that there was never going to be one — that the arithmetic would always be available and always be right, and that the only part which mattered was a decision that no amount of being right could ever make for you.

He wondered, sitting on the floor at twenty past two in the morning, whether one could build a machine that would do this part too. Not the counting. The other part.

It would be a considerable advance. It would take the fourteen hours off a man and put them somewhere they did not hurt.

And it occurred to him, with the lamp on and the bombes turning over behind the wall and forty-one ships making eight knots toward a hundred and ten miles of open water, that he did not know whether such a machine would be a mercy or the end of everything, and that he could not construct an argument that settled it, and that somebody was going to build one anyway, long after he was dead, and would not think to ask.

---

The signal went out at 04:40 that no action was to be taken.

The convoy held its course. The weather was fine. Visibility was very good.

Turing was asleep on the floor by then, which was not unusual, and Joan Clarke put a coat over him, which was.
