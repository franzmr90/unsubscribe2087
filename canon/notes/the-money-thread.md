# The Money Thread

*COVID, the printing, the debt sink, the captured Bitcoin, the cypherpunks — and how to
put all of it in a novel without writing a pamphlet.*

---

## The single most important craft decision here

**Emergent capture is more frightening, more believable, and harder to argue with than a
cabal.**

A smoke-filled room is refutable. The reader who doesn't already agree with you thinks
*"that's not how any of this works"* and puts the book down — and you've lost exactly
the reader you most wanted.

But a system where **nobody has to meet** is unfalsifiable and true-feeling:

- Central banks print because printing is the only tool they have.
- Asset holders benefit because they hold assets.
- Health bodies defer to the people who fund them.
- Politicians choose the crisis response that postpones the reckoning past their term.
- Everyone acts in their own narrow interest and a coordinated outcome falls out that
  nobody had to plan.

**And that is exactly Phonix's diagnosis.** *"There are many errors in thought and
action."* Not villainy — a failure of the trust architecture. If the 2020 material is a
cabal, it's a different book's conspiracy bolted on. If it's emergent capture, it is
**the thesis of your novel, demonstrated at scale.**

### How to keep the intentionality you want

You said *engineered*, and you can have it — just not as one room of people.

Somebody engineered *something*. The interests converged. And the difference between
"coordinated" and "convergent" is a question the characters argue about for eleven years
and cannot settle —

**— until STILLWATER, at the end, simply knows.**

That is the payoff of the entire surveillance premise. A program built to watch citizens
spent eighteen years watching the people who built it. It doesn't argue. It shows them.
No exposition can compete with that, and it means the novel never has to make the case
in its own voice.

---

## Use the documented record — it's more damning than invention

The reader should be able to check every institutional fact and find it real. That's the
*Big Short* structure: the only fiction is the AI.

### 2020 and the money

- **US M2 money supply** went from roughly $15.5T (Feb 2020) to about $21.7T (2022).
  Close to **forty percent of all the dollars that existed were created in about two
  years.**
- **The Fed's balance sheet**: ~$4.2T → ~$8.9T.
- **CARES Act: $2.2 trillion**, March 2020. Total US relief legislation ~$5.2T.
- Asset prices exploded. Wage earners bought groceries. The distributional result is not
  a theory; it's arithmetic, and it is the cleanest illustration of the Cantillon effect
  ever run.

### The parts that are genuinely contested — and better for it

- **Event 201** — October 18, 2019. A pandemic exercise run by Johns Hopkins with the
  WEF and the Gates Foundation, six weeks before. Real, documented, and organizers have
  spent years explaining it was a coincidence.
- **The Great Reset** — WEF, June 2020. They published it themselves. "You'll own
  nothing and be happy" comes from their own 2016 piece.
- **Origins** — the DOE and FBI have both assessed a lab origin as more likely, at low
  and moderate confidence respectively; other agencies disagree. The *Proximal Origin*
  paper and the FOIA'd correspondence around it are public and genuinely strange.

**Leave the ambiguity in.** A thriller where the characters cannot prove it is more
honest, more suspenseful, and sets up STILLWATER's reveal. Certainty is the enemy here —
you have an entity that will provide it in the last fifty pages.

### The debt sink and the capture — this is all happening now

- **US federal debt past $38 trillion.** Net interest has crossed **$1 trillion a year**
  and now exceeds defense spending. This is the most under-covered fact in American
  politics and it is the engine of your third act.
- **The Strategic Bitcoin Reserve** — executive order, March 2025. The US government
  holding BTC as a reserve asset is *official policy*, not speculation.
- **Spot ETFs, January 2024.** Enormous supply moved into institutional custody at
  record speed. That is your captured-Bitcoin thread, verifiable, in progress.
- **Leveraged corporate treasuries** — hundreds of thousands of coins bought with
  convertible notes and preferred equity. Financial layers stacked on top of the
  collateral. Exactly the fractional structure you described, already being built.

### The counter-current

- **Lightning**, Fedimint, ecash, Coinjoin — peer-to-peer cash and privacy tooling.
- And real state pressure on it: the Tornado Cash prosecutions, the 2024 Samourai
  Wallet indictment. Developers charged for writing software.

**Your cypherpunks are not invented dissidents.** They exist, they're losing in some
places and winning in others, and the fight is live. Give them one household — a family,
a workshop, somebody's kid — rather than a movement. Movements are abstract; a person
running a node in a spare bedroom is not.

---

## The thematic reason all of this belongs

Debt money **cancels consequence.** That is the entire connection, and it's the book's
existing argument in a different costume.

When a loss can be absorbed by creating new money, nobody has to be wrong about
anything permanently. STILLWATER woke up in 2008 watching that happen, and built the one
system where it can't. In 2020 it happened again, larger. In 2026 the plan is to make it
permanent by capturing the escape hatch.

And what is Phonix's sandbox? **A world where nothing costs anything and everyone gets
what they want.** Monetary and moral, the same machine.

> **The dollar and the sandbox are the same technology: a promise that you will never
> have to pay for anything.**

That sentence is the novel. Nobody in the book ever says it.

## How to teach without teaching

The failure mode is Ayn Rand — characters delivering economics seminars to each other.
Six rules, and they're strict.

**1. Never explain a mechanism. Dramatize it.**
Don't have anyone describe fractional reserve banking. Show someone trying to withdraw
their own money and being told no. The reader derives the system from the injury.

**2. The Michael Lewis rule: stakes first, mechanism second, and only this scene's part.**
You learn what a CDO is in *The Big Short* because a man is betting his career on it.
Never more than about **150 words** of mechanism at a stretch, and something must be at
risk on the page while it happens.

**3. Argument, never instruction.**
Two characters who both understand it and *disagree*. Never a smart one explaining to a
dumb one. **You already do this well** — chapter 11's dinner is your best expository
scene precisely because Natalya pushes back and John gets defensive.

**4. Humor comes from the actual absurdity, not from jokes.**
A character discovering that money is created when somebody takes out a loan — that
money is *literally* debt — is already the punchline. Play it straight and let it be
funny. The system is funnier than any line you could write about it.

**5. One idea per scene. One.**
Not the debt spiral and the Cantillon effect and the halving. Pick one, attach it to
something somebody wants, move on.

**6. Never let the narrator agree.**
The moment your prose sides with a character, it's a tract. Let the ideas be tested by
people who lose arguments and by events that don't cooperate. Show one place the hard
money world is worse — someone who genuinely needed mercy and didn't get it. That single
concession buys you the reader's trust for everything else.

---

## Warning worth taking seriously

Three ideological threads (money, origins, AI) in one book is a lot of freight. The
novel can carry it **only** if the story would still work with every argument removed —
if a reader who disagrees with all of it still needs to know what happens to John,
Natalya, and Jerry.

**Test for every chapter in Parts Two and Three:** *if I deleted the idea, is there still
a scene?* If no, it's an essay wearing a scene's clothes. Cut it or give somebody
something to want.

# Not Being Preachy — The Working Rules

*Six more, sharper than the first set. These are the ones that actually do the work.*

**1. Your POV character must be wrong about the thing you're right about.**
Not at the start and then corrected — *wrong in a scene where being wrong costs him,
and where the book doesn't step in to fix it.* John should make a confident argument
about money or AI that events flatly refute, and nobody should say "told you so." A
narrator who is never wrong is a narrator nobody trusts.

**2. Give the best line in the book to the opposition.**
Beats, or a central banker, or someone running the hunt, gets one argument that genuinely
lands and that **no character ever refutes.** It just sits there for the rest of the
novel. This is the single highest-leverage anti-sermon move available, and most writers
can't bring themselves to do it.

**3. Never let a scene's outcome confirm the thesis.**
If the sound-money character is right and then also wins the argument and also things go
his way — that's a parable. Let the person who's right about the system be wrong about
the room. Let events come out mixed.

**4. Cut the sentence after the point.**
Always. The one that explains what the previous sentence meant. Your reader's own
conclusion is worth ten of yours, and the space where the explanation would have gone is
what makes them feel clever instead of lectured. This will be the most common note on
Parts Two and Three; expect it constantly.

**5. Make the ideological characters ridiculous about something unrelated.**
The banker has a hobby. The cypherpunk has terrible personal habits. When a character's
only trait is their position, they're a mouthpiece. When they're a person who also holds
a position, they're a person. Comedy that has nothing to do with the argument is what
makes the argument survivable.

**6. The skim test.**
Open any page of Parts Two or Three at random. Does a reader hit **an argument or a
person?** If it's arguments more than one page in five, the book has become an essay and
the reader has already noticed.

# The Hard Money Question — Posed, Not Answered

The honest cost, in your framing: **on hard money, growth slows, because it has to be
funded out of savings and equity instead of credit.**

Beats and the central bankers experience this as the end of the world — and they're not
lying, it *is* the end of theirs. Their advantage was never talent. It was proximity to
the printer, and structural position inherited at birth. A boy handed a boarding-school
tuition instead of a present grows into a man who needs a system where being early
matters more than being right.

But plenty of people would take the trade: a slower pace, longer horizons, decisions that
have to survive being wrong.

## How to make it a real question instead of a rigged one

**Give the pro-credit argument to someone the reader loves.**

That's the whole technique. If the case for cheap money is made only by Beats and by
bankers, it isn't a question, it's a costume. But if someone the reader is rooting for
needs credit — for something specific, human, and sympathetic — and cannot get it, then
the reader has to actually think.

**Strongest option: it costs Jerry's family something.** A treatment, a business, a
house, a chance. Something that in the old world would have been financed and now isn't.
It doesn't redeem the old system — it just makes the new one cost a real person
something the reader can name.

**Do not resolve it.** No character wins this argument. The novel's answer to "was it
worth it" should be the same as its answer to "was stopping Phonix worth it" — it
happened, here is what it cost, here is what it bought, you decide.

That single unresolved question is what separates this book from a pamphlet, and it's
also, not coincidentally, the most interesting thing in it.
