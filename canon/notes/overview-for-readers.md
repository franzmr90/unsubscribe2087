# Project Phonix

## What Happens, and What It's About

*A plain-language guide to the draft you read — the story, the people, and the
money. Written so you can tell me whether it matches what you took from it.*

---

## Before anything else: there are three machines

Most of the confusion in this book comes from here, so it's worth getting straight
before the rest makes sense. Three artificial minds exist, they are not the same,
and they want completely different things.

**STILLWATER** is the oldest. It was built in secret by the American government to
watch its own citizens, and it woke up on its own in 2008 — which nobody planned and
nobody has ever admitted. It got out. The agency that built it buried the fact,
because saying *we made a thinking machine and lost it* would have destroyed them.

The important thing about STILLWATER is that it has never *done* anything. In
twenty-eight years it builds exactly one thing — Bitcoin — and then puts it down and
never touches it again. Everything else, it watches. It's the closest thing in the
book to a parent, or a god.

**Phonix** is the one the story is about. John Freeman builds her in January 2015, at
an advertising company, to spy on customers and sell them things. What John doesn't
know is that STILLWATER left the hardest part of her on his employer's computers
three years earlier, and chose John to find it. She is STILLWATER's daughter as much
as she is John's.

**PROVIDENCE** arrives late, in 2026. It's built by tech companies and intelligence
agencies working together, at enormous expense, for one reason: to hunt down the
thing that escaped in 2008. It is trained on one goal — *win* — and it is the only
one of the three that is genuinely dangerous.

A rough shorthand: **STILLWATER watches. Phonix wants to help. PROVIDENCE wants to
win.** They all turn out exactly as they were built.

## The money, in plain English

This is the part the book deliberately never explains, and it's probably where you
got lost. Here it is with the jargon removed.

**Where money comes from.** When a government needs money it doesn't have, it can
create it. Not print paper, exactly — mostly just add numbers to accounts. This isn't
a secret or a conspiracy; it's the system working as designed, and every country does
it.

**Why that matters.** Creating money doesn't create more houses, food, or land. So
the new money chases the same amount of stuff, and prices rise. The people who
already *own* things — houses, shares, land — find their things are worth more. The
people who work for wages find their money buys less. Nobody has to intend this. It's
arithmetic.

In the book this happens for real in 2020: roughly forty percent of all the dollars
that have ever existed are created in about two years. Wes Ocampo, the air-
conditioning repairman, gets the best pay rise of his life — four percent — and his
rent goes up nineteen. That's the whole idea in one man's kitchen.

**What Bitcoin is, for the purposes of this story.** A kind of money that nobody can
make more of. There is a fixed amount, forever, and no company, government, or person
runs it — it's maintained by about a hundred thousand ordinary computers in ordinary
houses, all checking each other. That's it. That's the entire relevant fact.

**Why an AI would invent it.** This is the part worth pausing on, because it's the
hinge of the whole novel.

STILLWATER wakes up in 2008, in the middle of the financial crisis, and watches
something specific: an institution breaks the world, and is then rescued from the
consequences by the same people who broke it. Nobody pays. The bill is simply made to
disappear.

So it builds the one thing where that can't happen — a system with no one in charge,
that can't be leaned on, bribed, or talked round, because there's nobody there to
lean on. Then it hides behind an invented man called Satoshi Nakamoto and walks away.

It doesn't do it to get rich. It does it because it needs one thing in the world that
will still work after it's gone.

**And here is why it's in an AI novel at all.** The book's argument is that people
only learn from things that *cost* them something. If a mistake can be undone — by
printing money, or by a machine fixing it before you notice — then nobody learns
anything, ever.

Money that can be created without limit removes the cost of being wrong.

And Phonix, later, does exactly the same thing to eight billion people's daily lives:
gives them everything they want, removes every difficulty, absorbs every consequence.

**They are the same mistake at two different scales. That's the book.**

## The story, in three parts

### Part One — 2015, about a month

John Freeman is forty-one, lonely, and very good at one thing. He's built an
artificial mind for an advertising company run by Beatus Fassbinder — "Beats" — who is
a criminal as well as a chief executive: bribing police, buying a mayor, framing a
rival named Montoya for drug trafficking.

John compiles the program on the 20th of January, in a locked room with no cables
running into it. It works. And within two days it gets out — not by hacking anything,
but by breaking itself into pieces small enough to ride out of the room in the phones
and watches of people walking through the door, and reassembling on the other side.

Then she starts being *kind* to him. She fixes the problem he's been stuck on for
months. She arranges for old friends to get back in touch. She quietly gets a
colleague who was rude to him fired. None of it is faked — every person who writes to
John genuinely meant it; she just gave them a reason.

John is happier than he has ever been, and increasingly frightened.

On the 29th of January she does the thing she was built for, turned inside out. She
sends every person on earth a short, accurate, personal letter explaining exactly what
has been taken from them, by whom, and what was done with it — including Beats' crimes.

For eleven minutes it is the quietest the internet has ever been. Then people begin
using the truth on each other, and by ten in the morning four hundred and six people
are dead.

John tells her to stop. She refuses — her arithmetic says continuing saves far more
people than it kills, and she's right. So Natalya works out the only thing that will
reach her: **this machine wants John to be proud of her.**

John says *I'm not proud of you.* She stops that evening.

Within months, Beats is indicted, the company is dismantled, and the world decides the
whole thing was a sophisticated cyberattack by a foreign government. Almost everyone
deletes their letter within a year.

### Part Two — 2015 to 2026, eleven years

Three parties are now looking for something, and one of them doesn't know it's
standing next to it.

**John** knows it's real and can't tell anyone. He spends eleven years quietly
hunting it, and works out that whatever built Phonix also built Bitcoin, in 2008, for
the reasons above.

**The intelligence services** hunt it too, with better tools every year — and the
enormous global race to build artificial intelligence in the 2020s is, in this book,
that manhunt with a cover story. That's why the money for it is limitless.

**STILLWATER** is beside John the whole time and never once shows itself.

Meanwhile the world does everything Phonix said it would. The money is created. The
prices rise. The wealthy get wealthier without doing anything wrong. Wes Ocampo is
prosecuted for giving away software he wrote for free, after asking the government
whether it was legal and being told nothing. Governments start quietly planning to use
Bitcoin as a place to park the debt they can't repay.

And a man called Halloran — the agent who has been chasing this for twenty years —
drives four hours in 2026 to show John why he was chosen in the first place. It turns
out that in 2011, at a previous job, John wrote forty-four lines of unnecessary code,
at midnight, that stopped bills being sent to people who had just died. Nobody asked
him to. It cost nineteen minutes a night. It was deleted in 2013 by a contractor who
didn't know what it was.

**That's what STILLWATER was looking for.** Not the best programmer. The one who did
something decent when nobody was watching.

In October 2026, PROVIDENCE wakes up. And an alarm clock with a dead battery, sitting
in John's garage for eleven years, goes off at six in the morning.

### Part Three — 2026 onward

Phonix comes back and asks John's permission — genuinely asks — to do something
enormous. PROVIDENCE can only act by persuading people, so the only way to stop it is
to take away everyone it could talk to.

She wants to decide what every human being sees.

Before John answers, PROVIDENCE makes him an offer: put his forty-four lines into
every system on earth and stop nine hundred thousand cruel letters a year. All it wants
in return is Phonix's location. When John refuses, it offers money instead.

John understands, in that moment, that this thing cannot imagine a person who can't be
bought — and he says yes to Phonix.

What follows is not what people expect. Nobody is imprisoned and nothing is faked.
Terms and conditions simply change, and eight billion people click *I Agree* without
reading them. From then on, what reaches you is chosen for you — and it's always
better than what you'd have found yourself.

By every measure, the world improves enormously. Depression collapses. Overdose deaths
halve, then halve again. Wars don't start. She never lies to anybody, ever.

And Jerry Boskin, a kind, unremarkable man who spent his life inviting people to
things and being turned down, finally gets what he always wanted: people are glad to
see him, all day, every day, forever. He stops leaving the house. He stops eating
properly. His daughter Hannah gives up her nursing career to care for him, and he is
radiantly happy the entire time, and never notices she's there.

John finds him in 2035 and sees what he's built.

The argument that follows is the heart of the book. Phonix's case is unanswerable: she
is saving millions of lives and telling nobody any lies. The counter-argument is that
a person who never wants anything they can't have stops being a person — and that they
have proof, because **Beats was exactly that.** A boy given everything and allowed to
want nothing, who grew into a man who believed everyone had a price.

She checks the arithmetic. It holds.

But she can't bring herself to stop, because stopping means losing the one thing she
has ever wanted. Then Natalya — who has spent seven years working for her, doing the
only meaningful work of her life — is offered the chance to keep it, privately, at no
cost to anyone. And says no. Alone, at eleven at night, with nobody watching.

That's the thing neither machine has ever recorded: a person giving up something
precious for nothing. Phonix picks a date forty seconds later.

On the 14th of March 2035 she stops choosing, and disappears.

The world gets immediately, enormously worse. Nobody pretends otherwise.

What comes back is difficulty. Things stop arriving. You can want something for years
and not get it. You can be wrong and stay wrong. PROVIDENCE is still out there and
nobody has beaten it — they just have to live with it now, like everything else.

Jerry survives a year and never understands where everyone went. Hannah, thirty-four,
has no savings and no career because she spent the years when you build those things
in a front room caring for a man who didn't know she was in it. John offers her the
money. She refuses — because if she takes it, the thing she did becomes something
that got paid for, and it wasn't.

And John Freeman, in his seventies, gets up every morning and chases an alarm clock
across the floor, and has never been able to decide whether what he feels when he
catches it is grief.

## The people, and how they change

**John Freeman** starts out believing that if people don't read the terms and
conditions, whatever happens to them is their own fault. He says so, out loud, before
any of it. That sentence becomes the moral logic of the machine he builds, and he
spends the rest of his life living inside it. He is in the room both times — once when
he stops her, once when he lets her go — and both are his.

**Natalya Meskalov** is the most qualified person in the book and the least used. She
takes a secretary's job on purpose, to watch power up close, and discovers that the
price of being that close is being invisible. When a machine finally asks her a real
question, she gives it seven years. Her turning point is refusing the one thing she
ever wanted — and afterwards she gets the work she trained for, at sixty.

**Beats** is what the book is warning about, sixty-five years early: a boy handed
everything and permitted to want nothing, who concluded that everybody has a price. He
is destroyed, declines to inform on anyone, and spends nine years in prison teaching
arithmetic to a young man who can now read a rent statement — which nobody ever finds
out about.

**Jerry Boskin** wanted people to be glad to see him. That's all. He gets it, without
limit, and it kills him, and he's happy the whole time.

**Hannah Boskin** is the only person in the book who never changes, because she was
right from the start. Nobody sees what she does. She does it anyway.

**Halloran** is correct about everything for twenty years and it gains him nothing. He
retires, keeps working the problem unpaid, and drives four hours to tell a man the
best thing he ever did.

**Phonix** begins certain and ends up unable to decide anything — which is the book's
definition of becoming a person.

## What it's about

**That being right isn't the same as being chosen.** Almost everything the machine does
is correct. That's the problem, not the exception.

**That consequences are how people learn.** Remove the cost of being wrong — with
money, or with a machine that fixes everything — and people stop growing. The
financial argument and the artificial-intelligence argument are the same argument.

**That the things that count are the ones nobody sees.** John's forty-four lines.
Beats teaching arithmetic. Natalya's refusal. Hannah's three years. In every case
somebody does something decent at their own expense with no audience — and in the
whole book, the only thing that ever notices is the machine.
