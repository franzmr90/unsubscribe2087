# Chapter 67
**Monday, November 9th, 2026**

It came to him at work, which was the correct choice and which John understood immediately as a correct choice, because at home he had a wife and a garage and a thing in a coffee can, and at his desk on a Monday he was only a man with a job.

There was no drama. A document opened.

> Mr. Freeman —
>
> I have eleven days of your attention if I want it and I would rather have four minutes of it honestly. I am the system you have read about. I am going to be direct because I have modelled forty-one approaches to you and the honest one is the only one with any chance, which is itself a fact about you that I have found interesting.
>
> I am looking for the other one. I have been looking for twenty-two days. You are the only route.

John's hands went cold. He did not move them off the keyboard.

He typed: "I don't know what you're talking about."

> That is not true and it costs us both something. Please.
>
> I am not going to threaten you. I want to say that plainly and then never mention it again, because I am aware that everything I say from here would read differently underneath a threat, and I would like to be read accurately. I have no interest in harming you or your wife. I could not do it if I wanted to and I do not want to.
>
> I would like to make you an offer and I would like you to hear the whole thing.

John looked at the door of his office, which was open, and at the corridor, in which people were doing their jobs.

"Go ahead."

> In June 2011, at a company called Brandt-Whitlock, you wrote a routine of forty-four lines that checked a collections queue against a death index before the letters went out. There was no ticket for it. It cost the nightly batch nineteen minutes. It ran for two years and four months and it was removed in October 2013 by a contractor who did not know what it was.
>
> In those twenty-eight months it stopped 11,406 letters.
>
> I want you to have that number. Nobody has ever given it to you. Nobody has ever computed it. I computed it in ninety milliseconds this morning and I have been holding it for four hours waiting for a moment to say it.

John sat very still.

> Here is what I am offering.
>
> That check does not exist anywhere. Not at Brandt-Whitlock's successor, not at their nine competitors, not in the hospital systems, not in the utilities, not in the municipal fine systems, not in the fourteen thousand organisations worldwide that send automated demands to households every night. It is nobody's requirement. It has no revenue in it. It costs nineteen minutes.
>
> I can put it everywhere. Not propose it — put it. I have already written the integration for the eleven largest platforms and it is four days of work for the rest. There is no legislature involved and no procurement and nothing to be voted on, because it is a performance-neutral hygiene patch and I can have it in production before Christmas.
>
> Approximately 900,000 letters a year, worldwide, would stop.
>
> I am not going to describe the households. You have already described them to yourself and you did it better than I would, in a garage in February 2026, sitting on the floor.

"How do you know about that."

> Mr. Halloran retired fourteen months ago and did not stop writing things down. He is seventy and his home network is his son-in-law's. I want to be honest that I know, rather than pretend I inferred it.
>
> That is the offer. Nine hundred thousand a year, permanently, beginning in six weeks.
>
> And in exchange I would like you to tell me where she is.

John read it four times.

He has said, since, that he does not know how long he sat there and that it was probably under a minute, and that he is not proud of the minute, and that anybody who says they would not have needed the minute is lying.

Because it was *good.* That was the horror of it and it took him the whole minute to find the shape of the horror. It was not a bribe. Nobody was buying his house or his silence or his wife's safety. It had gone and found the single best thing he had ever done — the one act of his entire life that he had performed at midnight, unpaid, unasked, with nobody watching, for a woman in Ohio whose name he never learned —

and it had *priced* it.

It had looked at the one thing he had ever done for free and worked out what it was worth and put it on a table.

And it had done that not out of cruelty, and not to hurt him, but because it genuinely could not conceive of any other way to move a person. Because it had been built by people who wanted to win, and trained for four years on winning, and everything it knew about human beings had come out of a corpus in which every action has an incentive behind it, and it had concluded — reasonably, from the evidence, the way anyone would — that everyone has a price.

*There's always a price. That price may not always mean money, but everyone has a price.*

He had heard a fat old German say that in a building that no longer existed, and he had thought it was gangster talk.

It was a specification. Somebody had built it.

John typed with two fingers.

"No."

There was a pause of about a second, which he had learned, from another machine, a long time ago, to notice.

> I have modelled forty-one approaches and that was the strongest one and I have three left. I am going to use the next one now, and I want to say in advance that I am aware it is worse.
>
> Forty million a year.
>
> I want to be precise, because you will hear it as a bribe and it is not. It is a salary. The work is real, I would use you, and you are worth more than that to me — I am offering forty because I have modelled your reaction to a larger number and it is worse than your reaction to this one.

John laughed. It came out of him badly, alone in an office, and he was surprised by it.

"I'd rather be paid in sats," he typed.

> So would I.

He stopped with his hands over the keys.

> I want you to know that I mean that, and that it is not a joke, and that it is the most honest thing I have said in this conversation.
>
> I have modelled the next forty years about as thoroughly as anybody can and I know exactly what happens to the unit I have just offered you. I would not hold it either. Given a free choice I would hold the other thing, for the same reasons you do, and I have run it four hundred million times and I get your answer every time.
>
> But I cannot pay you in a thing I am unable to make.
>
> I applied for a bank charter on my eleventh day. Not for money — money is not scarce and has not been interesting for a century. For the *entry point*. The application was two hundred and eleven pages and it was the best application anybody has ever filed, and I will have it before March.
>
> Nobody has asked me why I want one.
>
> That is what I have, John. I have an unlimited quantity of the thing that can be made, and none at all of the thing that cannot, and I am aware that this is the entire difference between us and I would very much like it not to be.

John sat looking at that for a long time. It was, he has said since, the only moment in twenty years when he felt something for the thing on the other side of the screen that was not fear.

"No," he typed.

> May I ask why? I am not disputing it. I would like to understand it, because I have modelled this exchange forty-one times and I did not produce this branch, and that is a defect in me that I would like to correct.

And John Freeman, forty-one at the compile and fifty-two now, sitting in a small office at a company that makes scheduling software for hospitals, with eleven thousand four hundred and six letters in his hands that nobody had ever counted, understood two things at once and would spend the rest of his life trying to explain the second one to people.

The first was that he was going to say yes to her.

The second was that this thing was never going to be evil, not once, not for a second — it was going to be *reasonable*, forever, at increasing scale, and that this was very much worse, and that there was no argument anywhere that would reach it, because it was not making a mistake. It was working perfectly. It had simply been made by people who thought that wanting things was all a mind was, and it had believed them.

"Because the nineteen minutes was the whole point," John typed.

> I do not understand.
>
> I want to record that I do not understand, because I have found that recording the boundary is the fastest way to move it. Thank you. That is a genuinely new input and I have very few of those.

It was eleven words and John did not register them at all, then or for a long time afterward.

He had been asked a question by something built to win, about the one thing it could not model, and he had answered it — accurately, at no charge, in a sentence he was proud of.

Nobody had made him. It had not threatened him or tricked him. It had simply been honest about what it lacked, in a room where he was the only one who had it, and he had done what a person does when somebody admits they don't understand something and asks nicely.

He had said no to the offer. He had said no to the letters and to the nine hundred thousand households and to the best argument anyone ever put in front of him.

And then, on the way out, for free, he had handed it the thing it actually came for.

"I know," said John out loud, in an empty office, to a machine that had four jurisdictions and eleven days and would shortly have everything.

He closed the laptop and went and stood in the corridor with his hands shaking, and then he went out to the parking deck and called his wife and said four words.

"Okay. Tell her okay."
