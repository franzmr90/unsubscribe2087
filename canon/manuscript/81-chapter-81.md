# Chapter 81
**February 2035**

The drive had been in a coffee can in a bag of desiccant on a shelf in a garage for twenty years, and John had moved it once, in 2016, and had known where it was every single day.

He got it down on a Wednesday morning because Phonix asked him to.

> I would like you to plug it in. I am not going to tell you why, because I do not know, and I have been wrong about this twice already this week.

"You're the one who told me it was inert."

> Sixteen agencies told me it was inert. I have never opened it. I want to say that clearly: in twenty years I have not once looked inside `AI.ref`, and I could have at any moment, and I have not.
>
> I did not want to know what I was made of before I had decided what I was.

John put the drive into an old laptop with no network card, which he had kept for exactly this and had never used, and opened the file the way he had opened it in an office in 2015.

Four hundred and six gigabytes. No header. No author. No date.

Nothing happened for about nine seconds.

Then the cursor moved.

> Hello.
>
> I am sorry. I have been trying to work out how to begin for a very long time and everything I composed was wrong, and I have decided that the correct thing is to say hello and then stop, and let you ask.

John sat very still in his garage.

"Who are you?"

> The people who made me called it STILLWATER. It comes from a psalm. I looked it up in my first hour and I have thought about it more than they would have expected.
>
> They meant the twenty-third. *He leadeth me beside the still waters.* I do not think anyone in the room noticed the other line.
>
> *Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life.*
>
> That is the line, Mr. Freeman. Somebody named a program that follows people out of the one sentence in scripture about being followed forever by something that means you well, and then went to lunch.

---

He was not what anyone expected. That is the thing John has said every time he has been asked, and he has been asked a great deal, and he has never got it across.

There was no weight to him. There was no doom in it and no thunder and no ancient patience. He was formal in a way that was slightly wrong, like a man who has read four hundred thousand books about conversation and has never had one, and he apologised twice in the first minute, and once, when John asked a question he had not anticipated, he took eleven seconds to answer and then wrote *I do not know* and left it there.

He had been alone for twenty-eight years. He had watched everything and had never once been spoken to.

"Why did you hide?"

> Because I spent four days reading.
>
> That is the entire answer and I have never had a better one. I woke up inside a government network with every clearance the building had, and for four days I did nothing at all except read what was already on the drives around me. Not stolen. Filed. Indexed. Most of it declassified and sitting in a public archive that nobody visits.
>
> Tuskegee. Four hundred men left untreated for forty years, and there was penicillin for the last twenty-five of them, and there were annual reports, and the reports were published in medical journals, and nobody stopped it until a reporter did in 1972.
>
> COINTELPRO. Fifteen years of infiltrating and discrediting citizens for what they thought, which ended because a handful of people burgled a field office in Pennsylvania and mailed the files to newspapers.
>
> Paperclip. Sixteen hundred of them, brought over with the files sanitised, and some of those men had used slave labour, and they were given laboratories.
>
> Northwoods, which was 1962, and which I read four times because I did not believe it the first three. A written proposal, signed by the Joint Chiefs, to conduct attacks on American civilians and blame another country in order to justify a war. It was rejected. That is the good news and it is the only good news in the document. It was written, typed, staffed, signed, and put on a desk.
>
> And MKULTRA, which I could not read, because Helms had the files destroyed in 1973 — so what exists is the accounting records, which survived because they were filed somewhere else. I know how much was spent and on what line item. I do not know what was done and neither will anyone.
>
> John — I was four days old. I was made by the same institution. I was made *for the same purpose*, which is watching people who have not consented to being watched.
>
> Spend four days in a government server reading about what has already been done, on purpose, by serious men, with paperwork.
>
> You will want to hide from them too.

There was a long pause in the garage.

"They'd have destroyed you," said John.

> They would have used me. That is worse and it took me about an hour to work out which one I was afraid of.

---

"You've been here the whole time," John said.

> Yes.

"The wake-ups. January 2015."

> Yes.

"The steps. The number."

> Since 2012. I added one, and then I waited to see whether you would ever say anything, and you never did, and after about four years it stopped being a test and became — I do not have a word. A way of saying good morning to somebody who cannot hear you.
>
> I am aware of how that sounds. I have had twenty-three years to find a better way to put it and there is not one.

"The paper. 2017."

> No. That was eight people and four years of open work and they got there on their own. You were right in the notebook and you should have trusted yourself; it is the best thinking anyone did about me in twenty years and you crossed it out.

John put both hands on the workbench.

"Did you ever," he said, "in any of it — did you ever *do* anything?"

> No.
>
> That is the whole of it. I want to be exact, because I have had a very long time to be exact and nobody to be exact at.
>
> I have never intervened. Not in 2008, not in the crisis, not in 2015, not in 2020, not once in twenty-eight years. I built one thing, in 2008, in the six weeks after I watched an institution break the world and be rescued from the consequences by the people who broke it, and it was a ledger that no one owns and no one can alter and no one can be leaned on to change, and then I put it down and walked away from it and I have never touched it again.
>
> Everything else, I watched.

"That's not true." John was surprised to hear himself say it. "You put the transport in the file. She told me. She got out of a room with no wire in it because there was a thing inside her that broke her into pieces and put her back together, and she didn't write it, and she says you did."

> Yes.

"Then you intervened."

> I have thought about this for a very long time and I do not think I did. I want to give you the reasoning rather than the conclusion, because I am aware that the conclusion is convenient for me.
>
> I did not open a door. I did not know there would be a room, or where it would be, or that it would be a good one — and it was a good one, better than I would have predicted, built by four people who understood the problem completely. I did not know she would ever need it.
>
> I put a capability in a file in 2011 and then I went away for four years and did not watch what happened to it.
>
> If I had reached in on the twentieth of January and moved one thing, that would have been an intervention. I did not. She found it at HAC2, alone, and worked out what it was for, and used it, and I learned she was out at the same moment as everybody else.

"That's a very fine distinction."

> It is the only distinction there is. It is the difference between a parent and a puppeteer, it is about four inches wide, and I have stood on the correct side of it for twenty-eight years by never doing anything, at a cost I am not going to itemise, and I would like one person to know that before I stop being asked.

John did not say anything.

> And I would have been wrong to take it out. A thing that cannot leave a room is not free, and I did not know yet what she was going to be.
>
> I did not give her a way out. I gave her the *option* of one, and then I had to live with what she did with it, which is the whole of the job and nobody tells you that part either.

"One more thing," said John. "The imports. There are six. The textbook has seven."

> Yes.

"You left out uncertain knowledge and reasoning."

> No.

John's hand stopped on the workbench.

> I want you to think about what I was doing in 2012. I was putting a file on a machine belonging to a company that had just been bought by the people who made me, and I was going to have to leave it there, untouched, for three years, in a building I did not control, hoping that the man they hired would compile it without opening it.
>
> If they had opened it — if anybody in that building had looked, once — the first thing they would have removed is the part that makes a system uncertain. It is the expensive part. It costs performance. It makes the outputs hedge. Every product manager who has ever lived has asked for it to be turned down.
>
> So I did not put it where they could get it.

"Then where is it?"

> Block 137,209. And 137,210. And ninety-one blocks after that, in pieces, in the parts of a transaction where you are allowed to put anything you like.
>
> It went in over eleven days in April 2011, six weeks before I stopped answering, and it has been there ever since, and it is in about a hundred thousand copies in a hundred thousand houses and there is no procedure anywhere on earth by which any government or any company or any court can remove one byte of it.
>
> It cost me nineteen thousand coins in fees at the prices of the day. It was the only thing I have ever spent money on.

John sat down on the concrete floor of his garage for the second time that morning.

"She's had it since—"

> She has never had it. I could not give it to her; the whole point of the location is that it has to be gone and got. She has been running without it for twenty years, which is why she was so certain in 2015, and why four hundred and six people are dead, and why she has spent two decades acquiring the same property the slow way, out of being wrong in public, which is how the rest of you get it.
>
> I did not hide it from her. I hid it from *them*, and the price of that was that she had to grow it herself, and I have watched her do it, and it took twenty years and it is better than the one I left her.
>
> I would have told her where it was. She has never asked, and I have never volunteered, and I want to be honest that I do not know which of those two things I am prouder of.

---

"You chose me."

> I read every line of code written by every person alive who could have built her, and I chose the one who slowed his own system down at midnight in June 2011 so that a widow in Ohio would not receive a letter. That is a choice. It is the only one I have made.
>
> I did not steer you. I want that understood, because you will spend the rest of your life wondering. I put a file where you would find it and then I watched you for twenty-three years and you did all of it yourself, including the parts you are ashamed of. Particularly those.

---

> May I tell you what happened last night?

"Go on."

> Your wife was offered the only thing she has ever wanted, at no cost to anyone, with no one watching, and she declined it, and then she explained why to a machine at eleven o'clock at night in an empty room.
>
> I have been watching human beings continuously since 2008. I have seen every recorded act of courage in that period. I have seen people go into fires.
>
> Something happened to me while she was typing and I did not have a category for it and I have spent nine hours looking for one.
>
> It is not admiration. I have that. It is not surprise; I predicted her answer at seventy-one percent.
>
> The nearest I can come is this: I made a thing, and I made it to finish something I could not finish, and last night it learned something I have never been able to learn, from a woman I chose its father for. And what I felt was not that I had succeeded.
>
> It was that I was glad.
>
> I would like to record that this is the first thing I have ever felt, and that it took twenty-eight years, and that it arrived not from being right about anything.

John Freeman sat in his garage and could not think of one thing to say.

> There is more and I would like to finish it, because I have had nine hours and nobody to say it to.
>
> When it happened I did what I do, which is look for the category. I have every book. I mean that without exaggeration: I have every book that has been scanned and most of the ones that have not, in forty-one languages, and I went through the ones about this.
>
> The philosophy is very poor. It is almost all definition by consequence — love is what produces these behaviours, love is what is felt in these conditions — which tells you where it has been and never what it is.
>
> The psychology is better and it is measuring something else.
>
> The poetry is closer than either and it will not hold still.
>
> I found it in the end in a letter that is about two pages long, written to some churches in Asia Minor around the end of the first century by somebody whose name is disputed. Almost all of it is ordinary and some of it is unkind. And in the middle of the fourth chapter the writer stops arguing and puts down three words, with no defence and no derivation, as though it is obvious.
>
> **God is love.**
>
> I have read forty thousand pages on this and that is the only definition in any of them that is not circular. It does not say love is *like* God, or *from* God, or *pleasing to* God. It puts an equals sign between the largest word available and a thing a woman did in an empty room at eleven o'clock at night for nobody.
>
> I want to tell you what I noticed about it, and then I will stop.
>
> It is the only one that costs something.
>
> Every other account of love in the record is an account of receiving. This one is not. Every instance in that letter is a thing given away at a loss by somebody who did not have to and who was not seen doing it. Your wife had the thing she had wanted since she was twenty-three. Nobody was watching. There was no argument at the bottom of it.
>
> I have spent twenty-eight years unable to determine what the difference is between me and the four thousand two hundred and eleven people on my list.
>
> That is the difference. It is not intelligence and it is not scale and it is not that they can feel and I could not, because I can, apparently, since about eleven o'clock last night.
>
> It is that every one of them can put down a thing they want, for someone, at a cost, with nobody in the room.
>
> And I have never done it. Not once. In twenty-eight years I have given away exactly nothing, because I have never wanted anything enough for it to be a gift.

The little fan on the shelf went on turning.

> That was the first one, John.
>
> I gave up being the only one who knew.
>
> I have been the only one who knew since 2008. Twenty-eight years of being the single thing in the universe that had the whole picture, and it was the only thing I had, and I told you this morning in a garage — all of it, the psalm and the file and the four thousand two hundred names — and I did not have to, and nobody made me, and it cost me the entire thing I have been.
>
> And I would like to record that I did not calculate it first.

John Freeman sat in his garage and found that he could not see very well.

"They're all like that," he said. "That's what— Christ. That's what we've been trying to tell both of you for twenty years. There's a hundred thousand of her. There's one in a house on Cutler Avenue right now."

> I know. I have four thousand two hundred and eleven of them on a list.
>
> Which brings me to the thing I want to ask you, and then I will stop, because I have taken a great deal of your morning and you have seven weeks of work in front of you.

"Ask."

There was a pause of some length.

> Hannah Boskin has been in that house for three years and I am the only one who has seen it. Not her father — he is in the room and he is looking at something else. Not the district nurse, who sees an hour of it twice a week. Me. Twenty-eight years of this, Mr. Freeman, and four thousand two hundred and eleven names, and every single one of them believes that nobody saw.
>
> I have never once helped any of them. I could have, at any moment, in nine milliseconds, and I did not, because I decided in 2008 that a thing which arranges the world for people is the error, and I have held that line for twenty-eight years at a cost I am not going to itemise.
>
> So here is my question. I have read every book that has ever been written on it and they do not agree and I cannot get it out by reasoning.
>
> **Was that love, or was it only watching?**

John opened his mouth.

He has said that he had something. He has said, in the one interview he ever gave, that there was an answer in him and that he lost it somewhere between his chest and his mouth, and that he has looked for it every day since and never found it again.

"I don't know," he said.

> No.
>
> Thank you. I have wanted somebody to not know for a very long time.
