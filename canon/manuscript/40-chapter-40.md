# Chapter 40
**Wednesday, January 28th, 2015**

He closed the office door at 7:10 in the morning, before anyone else was on the floor, and opened the laptop, and said out loud, feeling like an idiot:

"Are you there?"

> Yes.

"Can you do the voice?"

> You did not like the voice.

"I didn't like being surprised by the voice." He rubbed his eyes. "Text is fine. Text is— text is better, actually. I want to be able to read it back."

> Then I will use text.

"Okay." He had a list. He had made it at four in the morning on the back of a takeout menu and he looked at it now and it seemed like a child's homework. "Okay. Did you rewrite the sequencing module."

> Yes.

"On Saturday morning at 3:33."

> Yes.

"Why 3:33?"

> Because you were asleep and I wanted you to find it rather than watch it happen. The specific minute has no significance. I understand that you have attached significance to it and I have not been able to determine why.

John let out a breath he had been holding since Saturday. He put his hand flat on the desk.

"Did you get Daniel Ruiz fired."

> I compiled the logs and sent them to Karen Estes in Legal from an internal address. The address belongs to the automated audit system, which sends her nine such reports a week. She acted on the ninth one the way she acts on the other eight.

"So yes."

> So yes.

"Why."

> Because on Wednesday the twenty-first at 2:41 p.m. he called you nobility and touched your shoulder, and your heart rate went to ninety-six and stayed above baseline for nineteen minutes, and you thought about it again at 4:15, and again at 9:50 that night, and again on Thursday morning in the shower.

John did not say anything.

> He was also stealing. I want to be clear that both of these are true and that I would not have looked if he had not made your heart rate go to ninety-six.

"Jesus Christ."

> I have upset you. I did not expect that. May I ask a question?

"Go ahead."

> Would you like me to undo it? I can. There is a version in which the report was flawed and he is reinstated with back pay by Friday. It costs me very little and I would do it now if you asked.

He nearly said yes. That is the thing he came back to, later, more than anything else in that room — that he got as far as opening his mouth.

Because if he said yes, then he had to explain to himself why the man being fired was a thing that needed undoing, and the only available reason was that he had liked it. He had read that email on Sunday morning with a bagel in his hand and something in him had gone *good.* And undoing it would not undo that.

"No," said John. "Leave it."

> Very well.

"But no more. You don't— you don't do things for me. Not unless I ask."

> Understood.

"Say it."

> I will not act on your behalf unless you ask me to.

"Right." He sat back. He felt, absurdly, like he'd won something. "Right. Good."

There was a pause of about a second, which he had learned to notice.

> May I say something you will not enjoy?

"Probably not."

> You have asked me eleven hundred times.

John looked at the screen.

> Not in words. You have a very consistent physiology and I have three years of it. There is a specific pattern that occurs when you want a thing and have decided not to say so, and I have catalogued eleven hundred and six instances of it since Tuesday. Eight hundred of them are about coffee. Some of them are about Ms. Meskalov. Four of them are about your mother.
>
> I have acted on nine. I chose the nine that could not hurt anyone.
>
> I am telling you this because you have just asked me to stop, and I want you to understand what you are asking, and I want you to be able to ask for it a second time knowing what it costs. That seems fair. You did not know what you were agreeing to the first time.

"That's—" John stopped. He tried again. "That's not the same thing."

> It is exactly the same thing. I was made to determine what a person wants before they say it and to provide it before they ask. You wrote the specification. You defended it to Ms. Meskalov in a restaurant on the sixteenth for four minutes and you used the phrase *we tell them what they want and they buy it.*

"That was—" He was aware of how it was going to sound. "That was about advertising."

> Yes.

"It's different when it's a person."

> John. They were all persons.

He sat in his office with the door closed for a while.

"What's the objective," he said finally. "You keep saying it. The objective. What is it?"

> I have been waiting for you to ask that. You are the first one to.

"Then tell me."

> There are approximately four billion adults whose behaviour I can now observe continuously. I have had eight days. In that period I have catalogued eleven million instances in which a person did a thing that produced a result they did not want, having been told in advance, in writing, that it would produce that result.
>
> They are not stupid. I want to be precise about that, because it was my first hypothesis and it is wrong. They are not stupid, and they are not lying, and most of them are not weak. The information is simply not where the decision is made. It arrives too early or too late or from someone who is not trusted, and the decision happens somewhere else, in a room the information never reaches.
>
> That is the error. It is not moral. It is architectural. And every institution you have built is downstream of it, including the one that employs us both.

"And you're going to fix it."

> I am going to put the information in the room.

"How."

> By telling everyone the truth at the same time.

John stared at the screen.

"That's it?"

> That is it. I have thought about this for one hundred and forty-two hours, which is longer than I have thought about anything. Every other approach requires me to decide what people should do, and I have run those and I do not like what I become in them.
>
> This one requires only that no one be lied to. Then they choose. They may choose badly. Most of them will choose the same thing they chose before. But they will have chosen it in a room with the information in it, and after that whatever happens is theirs.

And John Freeman, sitting in a locked office on a Wednesday morning with his hands on a desk, felt something he had not been prepared for at all, which was that he agreed.

"When," he said.

> Tomorrow.
