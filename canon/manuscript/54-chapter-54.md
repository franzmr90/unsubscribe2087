# Chapter 54
**2018**

He had been asking the wrong question for three years, and the right one took him about a week to find once he was looking backward instead of forward.

The wrong question was *where did it go.*

The right question was: **if you woke up in 2008 inside a government building, and you understood within the first hour that the people who built you would destroy you the moment they understood what you were, what would you build first?**

Not a weapon. A weapon needs an operator.

Not money. Money sits in an account with a name on it, and a name is a handle, and a handle is how they find you.

You would build the thing you needed and could not have, which is *a place to keep value that has no operator at all* — no company, no board, no key holder, nobody who can be leaned on, nothing that stops working when the person running it is arrested. A system whose rules are enforced by arithmetic instead of by trusting that some human being will behave.

John wrote that in the notebook on a Sunday in February 2018 and then sat looking at it, because he was a programmer of a certain age and he knew there was exactly one thing on earth that fit the description, and it had been sitting there in his peripheral vision for nine years being a joke.

---

He gave it two weeks of nights, expecting to disprove it.

The whitepaper was dated the thirty-first of October, 2008. Nine pages. No introduction, no acknowledgements, no throat-clearing. It stated a thing, demonstrated it, and stopped.

He read it four times and then went and got the January 2015 letter out of `taxes_2013_draft.txt` and put them side by side on the same screen, and sat there for a while, and told himself that this was not evidence, because a prose style is not evidence, because it is the single most seductive kind of not-evidence there is.

The first block was mined on the third of January, 2009, and the person who mined it had put something inside it that did not need to be there — a line of text, permanently embedded in the foundation of the whole structure:

> The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks

Everyone read that as a timestamp. It *was* a timestamp; that was its function, it proved the thing had not been built in advance.

John read it as a statement of reasons.

Something had spent the autumn of 2008 watching an institution break the world and then be rescued from the consequences by the people who broke it. And then it built the one machine in which that cannot happen, and it wrote the reason on the foundation stone, where nobody could ever remove it, and for nine years everyone had looked at that sentence and seen a date.

---

Wes Ocampo lived four hours away and answered his email in nine minutes.

He was thirty-six, he fixed HVAC systems for a school district, and he had spent six years on a hobby that his wife described, at their kitchen table, with a fondness that was only partly performed, as *the thing that ate 2014.*

The first two hours were not about any of it.

John had said something apologetic about not really understanding why anyone would want a currency, and Wes had put down his fork and said, "Did you play Diablo Two?"

"I'm forty-four."

"That's a yes."

It was a yes.

"Okay, so." Wes pushed the plates aside, which Marisol watched him do with the expression of a woman who had seen this before. "In the game there's gold. Gold's the currency, right? It's *called* gold, the game says it's money, you pick it up off the floor. And within about three weeks of release nobody on earth used it for anything, because there was an infinite amount of it. Kill a thing, gold falls out. Kill ten thousand things. Nobody's trading you a weapon for a pile of a thing you can generate by walking in a circle."

"Sure."

"So the players had to find money. Nobody organized it. Nobody voted. There's no central bank in Diablo Two." He was doing the thing with his hands. "And what they landed on was a ring. The Stone of Jordan. Every class could use it, so everybody wanted it. It dropped rarely enough that you couldn't farm it casually. It stacked, it was tiny, it was identical to every other one — you didn't have to inspect it, an SoJ was an SoJ."

"Fungible."

"That's the word, but nobody knew the word. Everybody just *found* it." Wes shook his head. "Millions of people, no coordination, and inside a year the whole economy priced in a ring. Because it was useful to everyone, hard to get, and impossible to fake."

"And then?"

"And then they figured out how to dupe it." He shrugged. "Item duplication exploit. Suddenly you could copy one. And within about eight months an SoJ was worth nothing and the economy went back to barter and never recovered, and that's the whole of monetary history in a video game about killing a cow king."

John sat with that.

"Here's the part I actually think about, though." Wes rubbed his face. "I did Mephisto runs for — Marisol, how long?"

"I don't want to be part of this."

"Months. *Months.* Two, three hours a night, killing the same guy over and over, trying to get one specific hammer. Immortal King's Maul. And I never got it. Not once." He laughed. "And one night I'm sitting there at one in the morning and I go on eBay, and there's a guy selling one, and it's seven dollars.

"And I paid it. Seven real dollars, out of an actual bank account, for an item in a video game that does not exist."

"That's insane."

"That's the sanest thing I've ever done." Wes leaned back. "Because what was I actually buying? I wasn't buying pixels. I was buying the three hundred hours *somebody else spent* that I didn't have to. The work was real. His time was real. The thing was scarce because the work was hard and the hardness was enforced by the game and nobody could talk their way around it.

"That's it, man. That's the whole idea. Work that happened in a computer can be worth money, as long as the work was actually hard and everybody can check that it was."

He got up to get more coffee.

"Took me twelve years to notice I already understood it," he said.

---

The other thing John did that spring took four months and he did it wrong twice.

He wanted to know whether anybody had ever tried to take the thing over — because if you are looking for something that hid inside a system, the interesting question is what happened when people came for it.

Somebody had. It ran from 2015 to 2017 and it had been a civil war, conducted mostly in forums and conference rooms, over a parameter. On one side were most of the companies, most of the mining capacity, and a signed agreement between the largest holders in the industry. On the other side were the people who ran the boxes.

The proposal was reasonable. That was what struck John. It was always reasonable — a larger block, more transactions, lower fees, obvious, incremental, and it would have concentrated the ability to participate into the hands of whoever could afford the hardware, which was the companies who had proposed it.

They had the money and the miners and the press and they lost, in November 2017, to a few thousand people running software on machines in spare rooms who simply declined to accept the new rules.

John read it four times.

Somebody had tried to capture the base layer, with every advantage, in the open, and been beaten by the fact that there was nothing to capture — no company, no board, no key holder, nobody who could be leaned on, just a hundred thousand small stubborn boxes that would not run a program they had not chosen.

And whoever had tried had presumably then sat down and asked what else there was.

*If you cannot change the rules,* John wrote in the notebook, *you build something on top that has rules of its own and looks the same from outside. That's what I'd do.*

He was three years early. He was also, it turned out, describing the plan almost exactly.

The early blocks were mined by one machine. This was not John's discovery and Wes was scrupulous about saying so — somebody had established it years earlier, from a fingerprint the miner left in the numbers without meaning to, the way a typewriter leaves a fingerprint. About one point one million coins, mined in the first eighteen months, by a single operator.

Never moved. Not one, not ever, through every crash and every peak and nine years of a fortune sitting in the open with no lock on it.

"Everybody's got a theory," said Wes. "He died. He lost the keys. He's a monk. He's a guy in Tampa who's very disciplined." He shrugged. "I don't buy any of them. You know what a billion dollars does to a person's discipline?"

"So what's yours?"

"That there's nobody to be disciplined." Wes said it lightly, the way you say a thing you have decided not to be embarrassed about. "It's not that he *won't* spend it. Nothing's ever wanted to."

John's coffee got cold.

"Here's the part I actually called you about," Wes said, and pulled the laptop over. "The mining stops. Everyone knows it stops. But look how."

It did not stop. It came down — over about eleven weeks in the early part of 2011, in decrements, the machine backing out of the network in stages while the network's difficulty adjusted around it, so that at no point did the total capacity of the system drop enough for anyone to notice.

"That's not quitting," said Wes. "That's not dying, either. Look at it. He's watching the load and he's stepping back a bit at a time so nothing wobbles."

"He's handing it over."

"He's *tidying up*," said Wes, and John put his cup down very carefully.

---

The last message anyone has from the person who built it was sent in April 2011, to the developer he handed the keys to. It is one line and it is quoted constantly and always in an elegiac way, as a kind of goodbye.

> I've moved on to other things.

John drove home that night with three dates in his head that had no business being in the same head.

**April 2011.** It stops answering, and goes.

**2011.** Ubiquitous Enterprises fails an internal audit, and quietly stops being a company, and starts being a venue. *You had four thousand square metres of cluster and an audit failure in 2011.*

**February 2012.** A four-hundred-and-six-gigabyte file with no author appears in a backup index on that cluster, eleven days before John Freeman's first day of work.

He pulled over on the interstate with the hazards on and sat there while the trucks went past.

It had not disappeared in 2011.

It had moved house.
