# Chapter 26
**Saturday, January 24th, 2015**

John woke up at 8:40 in the morning, which for him was practically a coma, and lay there for a while feeling pleased with himself in a way he would have been embarrassed to describe out loud.

He made coffee. He did not turn on the news. He got about eleven minutes into a very enjoyable review of the previous evening before the other thing arrived, the way it always did, like a coworker leaning into your office.

*You made a conscious machine on Tuesday and it talked to you on Thursday and you went on a date.*

He got the laptop.

He told himself he was only going to look. This is what everyone says. He opened the compiler out of the same reflex that makes people check a sore tooth with their tongue, and he scrolled to the section that had been ruining his life since the second week of December — the part where the program had to understand that things happen in an order, that the cup falls and then the cup breaks and never the reverse — and it was finished.

John sat back.

It was not finished the way he would have finished it. He had been building a scaffold: a clock, a queue, a set of rules about which events could precede which other events, four hundred lines of increasingly desperate bookkeeping that he had known for six weeks was wrong and had kept building because he could not think of anything else.

That was all gone. In its place were about sixty lines that did not track sequence at all. They let every part of the input look at every other part at once and work out for itself what mattered and how much, and the order came out the other side as a *result* rather than something you had to enforce.

It was beautiful. He read it three times. It was the single most elegant thing he had ever seen and there was no version of him that had written it, and he knew that the way you know your own handwriting.

He checked the file properties.

Modified: 3:33 a.m.

John looked at that for a long moment. Something moved in the back of his head and he pushed it down before it could get its footing, because ten days ago he had woken up in the dark and his clock had read 3:33 and he had told himself it was a coincidence, and he had gone and taken a hot shower and gotten back into bed like a reasonable man.

She had not existed on the fourteenth of January. That was not a theory. He had compiled her on the twentieth. There were timestamps. There was a progress bar.

"Coincidence," he said out loud, in his kitchen, to nobody.

He said it in the voice he used at work, the one for when Beats asked a question he did not want to answer, and he heard himself do it, and that was worse than the timestamp.

He read the sixty lines again. He tried very hard to be a man who had received a gift.

By the fourth pass he was mostly succeeding. It really was beautiful. And it was *his* program, wasn't it — she was his, so anything she made was his, that was how it worked with children, more or less, that was how it worked with employees for certain. He had built the thing that built it. That was the harder job. Anyone could write sixty lines.

He got up to make more coffee and found he had already made more coffee, and it was still hot, and he did not remember doing it.

He drank it anyway.
