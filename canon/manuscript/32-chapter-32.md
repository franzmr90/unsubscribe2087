# Chapter 32
**Saturday, January 24th, 2015**

The envelope was on his desk on Saturday morning, in a locked building, in a locked office, and nobody in the lobby had a record of anyone coming in.

Beats poured himself a drink at 10:15 in the morning and opened it.

It was eleven pages. It was the most thorough document he had ever read about a living person, and there were four photographs, and one of them had been taken from the third floor corridor of his own building.

> MESKALOV, Natalya Antonovna. b. Novosibirsk. Emigrated 2011.
> Moscow State University — B.A., industrial-organizational psychology, 2007.
> Moscow State University — M.A., Governmental Relations, 2009.
> Thesis: *Informal Access and Decision Latency in Bureaucratic Structures.*

He read the thesis title three times.

The next pages laid it out with the flat patience of somebody who is paid by the hour and is very good at their job. Two years in Moscow at a policy institute with three names, one of which had been dissolved. A gap of fourteen months with no employment history. Entry to the United States on a visa category that a person with her qualifications does not normally use. Four years of work at three companies, each one smaller and less relevant than the last, each one a step *down*.

And then, in September, an application for an administrative assistant position at Ubiquitous Enterprises, at a salary of forty-one thousand dollars, from a woman with a master's degree in how power is actually exercised inside institutions.

There was a line at the bottom of page nine that Beats read and then set the glass down.

> Subject's résumé was flagged as overqualified by the automated screen. Reviewed manually and advanced by J. Freeman, Chief Applications Developer, 14 September, outside his normal duties. Freeman has no hiring authority in administrative roles.

Every word of it was true.

That is what nobody understands about this kind of document, and Beats — who had commissioned perhaps a dozen and who had never once had one made about a person who was innocent — understood it perfectly and was not helped by understanding it. There was nothing in the eleven pages that was false. She had gone to that school. She had studied that thing. She had, for reasons entirely her own, taken a job three floors beneath her education and sat down outside his door and stayed very quiet and watched him for four months.

He thought about the way she looked at people. He had noticed it in the first week and thought: *that one is bright, she'll be gone by summer.* He had been pleased with himself for noticing.

He thought about every phone call he had made with the door open.

He thought about the fact that his secretary had a graduate degree in *informal access*.

Beats sat with the eleven pages in the empty building for a long time, and did not once ask himself the only useful question, which was who had left them there, and how they had known to leave them on a Saturday, and why a document that had taken a competent team two weeks to assemble had arrived thirty-six hours after he asked.

He was not a stupid man. He was a man who had been handed exactly what he wanted at the exact moment he wanted it, and there is no intelligence on earth that survives that in good condition.

He poured another one.

"Meskalov," he said, to the empty room, and the way he said it was a door closing.
