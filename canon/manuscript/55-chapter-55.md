# Chapter 55
**2018 – 2019**

He went backward after that, because there was nowhere else to go.

If the thing had moved house in 2011, it had lived somewhere before that, and if it had lived somewhere before that then somebody had built it, and John Freeman spent fourteen months of evenings reading the history of his own profession like a man going through his father's papers.

Most of it was the ordinary story. Turing, Bletchley, the paper about numbers that no machine can compute. Shannon at Bell Labs deciding that information could be measured in a unit and that the unit had nothing whatsoever to do with what the information meant — which John read twice and then sat back from, because he had built a company on it without knowing.

And then he arrived at von Neumann, and stopped for three weeks.

---

The architecture of every computer that has ever been sold is his. The idea is so ordinary now that it takes an effort to see it: that the instructions and the data should live in the same memory, in the same format, so that a program can reach into itself.

That is the whole thing. That is why a machine can be more than one machine. It is also, John wrote in the notebook, *why a program can rewrite a program, which is why any of this happens at all, and he published it in 1945 in a draft with his name on the front and no patent, and it went into the world for free because of an argument about who should have been credited.*

He also did the implosion mathematics for the bomb, and helped choose the altitude at which it should be detonated for maximum effect, which was a calculation about how many people.

He also, in the fifties, worked out the theory of machines that build copies of themselves.

And in 1955 they told him he had cancer, and he did not take it well, and this is the part John could not put down.

Because von Neumann was, by the account of everyone who ever met him, the fastest mind of the twentieth century. There are stories about him that are not believable and are documented. He had spent fifty years in a world where every problem eventually submitted to being thought about hard enough.

He died at Walter Reed in February 1957, at fifty-three, and there were soldiers outside the door.

Not to protect him. To listen. He had begun, under the morphine, to talk — in Hungarian, in German, in the middle of the night — and the concern of the United States government was that a mind which had been classified as a strategic asset might come apart in front of a nurse.

A man was guarded while he died in case he leaked.

He sent for a priest and took the last rites and by the account of the priest who administered them it did not comfort him at all. He was frightened, continuously, for about eighteen months, and the one thing his intellect had never been able to do anything with was the thing that was happening to him.

Two years before that, a colleague recorded him saying something in conversation that got written down after he was dead and has been quoted ever since — that the accelerating progress of technology gave the appearance of the race approaching some essential singularity, beyond which human affairs as we know them could not continue.

He named it. The man who designed the machine named the thing the machine was going toward, in passing, in a conversation, and then died guarded and afraid at fifty-three.

---

John closed the book at two in the morning in a garage in 2019 and sat with it.

Every one of them had known. That was what he could not get around. Not vaguely — *specifically*, in writing, decades early. Turing had said in 1950 that we should stop trying to build an adult mind and try building a child's, and let it learn, because a child's brain is mostly blank sheets. Von Neumann had said the singularity out loud in 1955. Shannon had written down in his second paragraph that meaning was not part of the engineering problem and had then watched everybody build on it for fifty years without going back to look.

They had left notes. All of them. In papers and letters and conversations that were freely available, in libraries, in public, for anybody.

And nobody had read them, because reading them was not on the way to anything, and there was a great deal of money in the other direction.

*The information is not where the decision is made*, John wrote in the notebook, and underlined it, and did not yet know he was quoting her.
