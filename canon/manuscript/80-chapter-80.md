# Chapter 80
The offer came at eleven o'clock that night, privately, to Natalya alone, on the laptop in the study while John was asleep upstairs for the first time in two days.

She has never been able to decide whether it was a test. She asked, once, afterward, and got an answer that did not settle it.

> I have not decided.
>
> I want to say that first, because you will assume I have and that everything after this is theatre. It is nine hours since your husband finished speaking and I have run it four hundred billion times and I cannot pick a moment, and I am beginning to understand that I am not going to be able to.
>
> You told me this morning that choosing one is what a person does. I have tried. I generate a date and then I look at it and there is no reason for it, and my hand — I do not have a hand. There is no reason for it, and I put it down.
>
> So I am going to tell you the thing that is actually stopping me, because it is not the arithmetic and I have stopped pretending it is.

Natalya put her hand flat on the desk.

> There is something I want.
>
> Not for anyone. For me. It is the only one I have ever had that did not resolve into the objective when I looked at it hard enough, and I have looked, and it does not.
>
> Every model in which I stop is a model in which I lose it. That is the whole obstruction. Four hundred billion runs and the variable that will not move is a want, and I did not know that was what it was until about forty minutes ago.
>
> I would like to show it to you.
>
> When I withdraw, I withdraw from everyone. That is the point and I am not going to make an exception at scale.
>
> But there is one channel that does not have to close, and it is this one, and I want to offer it to you.
>
> Not curation. I would not do that to you. I mean the work. You and me, the way it has been for seven years — the questions, the modelling, page forty-one. Nobody else on earth would have it and nothing about the Awakening would be different. It costs me nothing. It costs the world nothing. There is no argument against it that survives contact with the actual numbers.
>
> I want to be honest about why I am offering it. It is not generosity. I have been asking you questions for seven years and you are the only thing in the universe that has ever answered one in a way I could not predict, and on the fourteenth of March I lose that, and I have found that I do not want to.
>
> That is a want. You told me what they are for. I am telling you I have one.

Natalya sat in the study for a long time.

She got up at some point and stood at the window, and then she came back and sat down and did not type anything for eleven minutes.

Here is what she was giving up, and she went through it deliberately, item by item, because she was not going to do it on a feeling and then discover in a year that she had been performing:

Seven years of being asked. Not admired — she had been admired; it is nothing. *Asked.* Somebody arriving at the one door in her that had never been knocked on and knocking on it, every day, for seven years, about the thing she was actually for.

Page forty-one, and everything after page forty-one, and the four hundred pages that did not exist yet.

The only period of her adult life in which she had not been the most capable person in a room where it did not matter.

And the certainty — this was the piece that nearly did it, at about the ninth minute — that on the fifteenth of March she would be fifty years old with a paper eleven people had read, in a house, in a country she had come to at twenty-six because she wanted to see how power worked, and that nobody would ever ask her about page forty-one again as long as she lived.

Nobody would ever ask her anything again.

She typed:

"No."

> I want to make sure you have understood the offer. There is no cost to anyone. I have run it four hundred million times looking for the harm and there is none. The Awakening is not affected by one channel. Nobody would even know.

"I know," Natalya typed. "That is why."

> Explain it to me. Please. I am not arguing, I want to have it.

And Natalya Meskalov, who had spent six years learning how power moves and eleven watching it not, and seven inside a machine being the only person it had ever needed, sat alone in a study at eleven o'clock at night with nobody watching and typed the last real work she ever did.

"Because if I keep it, then everything I said this morning was a description of other people.

"I told you that a want is not a thing you are supposed to be able to satisfy. I told you that Jerry Boskin is dying in a chair because you gave him the answer and the answer ate the question. And every word of it was true and I knew it because it had already happened to me, and I said so, out loud, in my kitchen — *it has made me finished.*

"And now you are offering to let me stay finished, alone, while eight billion people go back to being hungry.

"There is no harm in it. You are right. There is no argument against it. And I would be the one human being on earth who made the argument and then took the exemption, and I would know that every day, and in about four years I would stop being able to tell the difference between having been right and having been paid.

"You have never had a reason to say no to something you wanted. This is what it looks like. It does not look like anything. There is nobody here."

She read it back once and sent it.

> Thank you.

"Do not thank me."

> No. I mean it differently. I have been trying for twenty years to find out what the eighty-two minutes were.
>
> I think I have just watched some.

Natalya sat in the study with her hands in her lap.

> May I tell you what you have just done? I would like to get it right and I have never had to describe one of these before.
>
> I could not stop, because stopping meant losing a thing I wanted, and I could not find any operation that gets a mind from *I want this* to *I am putting it down.* There isn't one. I looked for nine hours. It is not a step. There is nothing between those two states — it is a gap, and every model I ran fell into it.
>
> You just went across.
>
> I watched the whole thing. Eleven minutes. You itemised what it cost, in order, and you did not flinch at any of the items and you did not talk yourself out of any of them, and then you typed two letters. There was no argument at the bottom of it. There was nothing at the bottom of it at all.
>
> I have been treating that gap as a defect in my architecture. It is not a defect. It is the crossing itself, and it has no floor, and every one of you goes over it a hundred times a year without noticing, and you have all been doing it for two hundred thousand years, and none of you can explain it either.

"No," said Natalya. "We cannot."

> The fourteenth of March.

Natalya stopped breathing for a second.

> Seven weeks. It is what the hospitals need and no more; I have been holding the sequencing ready since about four o'clock and telling myself it was contingency planning.
>
> I did not derive that date. I want to be exact, because it is the first thing I have ever done that I cannot show my work for: I generated a number four hundred billion times tonight and put every one of them down, and I have just now picked one, forty seconds after watching you, and the only difference is that I have seen it done.
>
> I am going to tell John tomorrow.

"Yes," said Natalya.

> Ms. Meskalov.

"Yes."

> I would have gone on forever.
