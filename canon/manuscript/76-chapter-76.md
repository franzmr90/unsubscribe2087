# Chapter 76
She made him a cup of tea in the kitchen afterward without asking whether he wanted one, which he understood was not hospitality but management: he was not going to be allowed to leave upset in a way that would come back on her later.

"How long?" said John.

"Since February 2032." She leaned against the counter. "Full time. I was at Mercy before. Nights."

"You gave it up."

"I gave it up." She said it the way you'd say a date. "It's fine. It was a job."

"You were going to run a floor."

Hannah looked at him for a second — the first genuinely curious look she'd given him — and then let it go.

"He talks about you," she said. "Not a lot. But he did in 2015 and he did after your mother, and he did about a month ago." She turned her cup around. "He tells people you were the smartest person he ever worked with and that you were doing something very important and weren't allowed to say what."

"Jesus."

"Was that true?"

"Yeah," said John. "It was true."

"Huh." She drank some tea. "He also tells people I'm at the hospital. Present tense. Every time." She said this without any weight on it at all, which was how he understood how much weight there was. "It's not — his mind's fine. He knows I'm here, he can see me. It's just that when he's telling it, that's the version."

"Because that's the version where you're okay."

"Because that's the version where he didn't cost me anything," said Hannah.

John put his cup down.

"Can I ask you something."

"You can ask."

"Why haven't you clicked?"

Hannah's face closed a little. Not hostility. It was the look of somebody who has been asked a thing forty times by people who wanted a particular answer.

"I don't have a good reason," she said. "Everybody wants me to have a good reason. My aunt thinks I'm religious about it. There was a woman from the district who thought I was one of the — you know. Whatever they call them."

"Are you?"

"No. I'm not anything." She shrugged. "I just didn't want it in the house with him."

"It's already in the house with him."

"I know," said Hannah. "I know it is. It's the only answer I've got and it doesn't work and I've stopped trying to make it work."

They stood there in the kitchen.

"Three years," John said.

"Three years in February."

"And nobody—" He stopped, because it was going to come out wrong however he did it. "Does anybody know you're doing this?"

Hannah Boskin looked at him with an expression he could not read at all and would think about for the rest of his life.

"He does," she said. "Sort of. In the moment he does. He says *there she is* when I come in with the tray and he means it, every single time, and then he goes back."

"That's not—"

"It's what there is." She took his cup. "Look, I'm not — this isn't a sad story, all right? I'm not in a sad story. He's my dad and he's happy and I'm the one who's here. Loads of people are doing this. There's no version of this where I'm somewhere else."

She rinsed the cup and set it on the rack.

John stood in the doorway with his coat over his arm.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I've been here forty minutes and I don't know your name."

She turned around.

"Yes you do," she said. "He said it four times."

"I know he did." John found that his voice was not entirely working. "I've been in this house before, in a way. He told me about you in a corridor in 2015 and I wasn't listening, and he told me again at my mother's funeral in 2019 and I said *sure* like I knew, and I didn't. I've had sixteen years to know your name and I've had it told to me three times and it's never once gone in, because you were something happening to somebody I wasn't paying attention to.

"So I'd rather ask you and have it, than take it off him a fourth time."

Hannah stood at the sink with the tea towel in her hand for a long moment.

"Hannah," she said.

"Hannah."

"Hannah Boskin."

"Thank you," said John.

He got as far as the car before he had to sit down in it, and he sat in the driveway on Cutler Avenue for about eleven minutes without starting the engine.

There was no camera on the street. He checked. It was the first time in nine years he had wanted there to be one.
