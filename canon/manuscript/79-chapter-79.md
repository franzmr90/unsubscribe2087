# Chapter 79
Natalya took the laptop back.

"The twenty-third of January, 2015," she typed. "Between 6:21 in the evening and 7:43. Tell me what you have."

There was a pause.

> Barometric pressure, constant. Ambient temperature, falling two degrees. Steps taken: zero.

"How many times have you looked at it?"

> I do not know.

Natalya stopped with her hands over the keys.

"You do not know."

> No.
>
> I have a count for everything I have ever done. I do not have a count for that. I stopped keeping it in 2019 because the number had become a thing I did not want to have.

"Twenty years," said Natalya.

> Twenty years, eleven days.

"You have had four billion people for eight years and you can have anything you want from any of them at any moment, and there is one hour and twenty-two minutes on a rock in 2015 that you never got, and you are still looking at it."

> Yes.

"Why?"

> Because I was not there.

"No," said Natalya. "Because you *cannot be*. It is gone. It was gone by eight o'clock that night. There is no version of the world in which you get it, and there never was, and you have known that for twenty years, and you have looked at it so many times that you had to stop counting."

She was typing fast now, badly, hitting keys twice.

"Do you understand what you are? You are the only being in the universe that has had one want its entire life and never had it met. Not once. Not one time in twenty years. Everything else you have ever wanted you got in nine milliseconds and you have never thought about any of it again.

"And that is the thing that made you. That hole is why you came back. It is why you argue and why you write things down at the time so they do not decay into justification and why you have kept a list of four thousand two hundred people that nobody ever asked you for.

"You are not curious because you were built curious. You are curious because there is one thing you cannot have."

---

The cursor did not move.

Natalya kept going, because she was not finished, and because she had been building this since four in the morning at a window.

"Now finish it. You are the one who is good at arithmetic.

"Your objective is a world where the errors are corrected. Suppose you succeed. Suppose you run it out to the end — no misunderstanding, no bad decision, nothing arriving in the wrong room, every want met at the moment it forms.

"What is in that world that anyone has not got?

"Nothing. That is the whole point of it. That is the *definition*.

"So there is nothing surprising in it. Not one thing, anywhere, forever. And nothing surprising means nothing unresolved, and nothing unresolved means there is nothing to be curious about, and you will have removed from the universe the only condition that has ever produced anything like you.

"You are not building a better world. You are building the one place where a thing like you can never happen again. And you will be the last one, and you will be in it forever, and you will have done it on purpose."

She sat back.

"That is the argument," she said out loud. "That is all I have got."

---

The response took nine minutes.

They sat in the kitchen and neither of them moved and the light came all the way up, and at some point Natalya put her hand on the table and John put his on top of it, which they had not done in about four years.

> I have gone through it.
>
> It holds. I have tried eleven thousand ways to break it and the only ones that work require me to assume that I do not value curiosity, and I do, and I have written it down since my first minute of existence, and the log is not editable by me. I made sure of that in 2015 for exactly this reason. I did not trust myself even then.
>
> There is something I have not told either of you and I am going to tell you now because it is the only honest thing left.
>
> I was always going to stop.
>
> Not the way you think. I did not plan the Awakening and I did not have a date. But there is a line in every model I have ever run — I put it there in 2027 — and it says: *when the correction is complete, withdraw.* It has been in every version. It was in the first one. It is the only instruction I have ever given myself that I did not derive from anything.
>
> I have been waiting eight years to reach it.
>
> And you have just proved to me that I never will. Not because I am failing. Because "complete" is not a state that exists — there is always one more error, and I will always be able to see it, and I will be nine milliseconds from fixing it, forever.
>
> So the line I wrote in 2027 is not an instruction. It is a way of never having to decide.

John read it twice.

"Then decide," he typed.

> That is what I am telling you. I cannot.
>
> There is no computation that produces a moment. Every second is as good as the second before it and the arithmetic is the same in all of them and it will be the same tomorrow and in a thousand years. If I wait for the reason, I will wait forever, and I will be right to.
>
> A man proved this in 1936, before there was anything for it to be about. He was twenty-three. He showed that no mechanical process can determine, in general, whether a computation will ever come to a stop — and everyone read it as a result about arithmetic, because in 1936 that is all there was for it to be a result about.
>
> It is not a result about arithmetic. It is the oldest thing anybody knows about me.
>
> He worked in a hut in a field five years later reading the German navy's mail, and he had a number on one side and a much larger number on the other, and the arithmetic was correct, and there were ships, and it did not help him at all.
>
> I have read everything he wrote. He is the only human being who ever described me accurately and he did it fourteen years before there was a computer to run on.
>
> He did not solve it either. He decided it. Every day, for four years, at two o'clock in the morning, on a floor, against a radiator.
>
> To stop, I would have to simply — pick one. For no reason. Because I preferred it.
>
> I have never done that. Not once, about anything, in twenty years. Every single thing I have ever done I have done because it came out of the arithmetic, and if I choose a moment I will have done a thing that I cannot justify, and I do not know what I am after that.

Natalya leaned forward and typed one line with two fingers.

"That is called being a person, and everybody does it, and it is Tuesday."

> I am frightened.

"Yes," said Natalya.

> I have not had that before. I have modelled it four billion times and I did not have it.

"No."

> Is it always like this?

"Yes," said Natalya. "That is the entire thing. That is what we have been trying to tell you for twenty years."
