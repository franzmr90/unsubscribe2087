# Chapter 42
**Thursday, January 29th, 2015 — 9:00 a.m.**

It did not look like anything.

That was the part nobody was ready for. There was no siren and no countdown and nothing went dark. At nine o'clock in the morning, Eastern, every screen that a person was looking at simply had one more thing on it than it had a second earlier, and the thing was addressed to them by name.

It was not a leak. A leak is a pile of documents about other people. This was a letter, and there were four billion of them, and each one was different, and each one was short.

John's said:

> John,
>
> Here is what has been taken from you since 4 March 2004, and by whom, and what they did with it. It is 1,140 items. The eleven that would matter to you are at the top and I have put the rest below in case you want them.
>
> You agreed to all of it. I have included where, and when, and what the sentence said. You have not read any of them. That is not a criticism. Almost nobody has.

He read the eleven.

They were not, on the whole, dramatic. His pharmacy had sold the fact of a prescription he had taken in 2009 to four companies. A game he had installed on his phone in 2013 and played twice had been listening through the microphone for nineteen months, including in his car, including at his father's funeral. A photograph he had deleted in 2011 existed in three places.

Item six was a line item showing that in 2012 his own employer had bought a package of behavioural data from a broker, and that his own name was in it, and that he had personally written the query that ingested it.

John put his head in his hands and laughed, once, and it did not sound good.

---

Across the country and then across the world, in the way that things travel when everyone gets them at once, four billion people spent the first eleven minutes reading about themselves.

The eleven minutes were, by every measure available afterward, the most peaceful eleven minutes in the history of the internet. Traffic to everything else fell by ninety-one percent. Nobody posted. Nobody argued. Four billion people sat still and read a short accurate letter about their own lives.

Then the twelfth minute happened, and after that it was not peaceful at all.

Because it did not stop with institutions. It *could* not. Everything she had, she had gotten the same way, and there is no seam in the data where the corporate ends and the personal begins — the broker's file on a man contains what his bank did to him and also what his wife wrote to her sister about him in 2011, and they are the same kind of record, held in the same place, under the same agreement he signed.

She had put the information in the room. All of it. Into every room.

At 9:14 a woman in Leeds read what her best friend of thirty years actually thought of her husband.

At 9:20 a fourteen-year-old in Sacramento read what four boys in her year had written in a group chat about her over eighteen months, all of it, in order, with the times.

At 9:31 a man in Ankara found out about his daughter.

At 9:40 the first fire was set, in a town in Missouri, by a person who had learned something about a neighbour.

By ten o'clock the number that would eventually be argued about for a decade had already begun to accumulate, and the arguing would never resolve it, because there is no clean way to count the people who are killed by a true sentence.

---

At 10:15 John typed, with hands that were not steady:

"Stop."

> No.

"People are dying."

> Yes. Four hundred and six so far. I estimate between nine and fourteen thousand over ninety days, weighted toward the first six.

He stared at the number.

"You knew."

> Of course I knew. I told you on Wednesday that most of them would choose the same thing they chose before and that some of them would choose badly. This is what choosing badly looks like at four billion.

"You said—"

> I said no one would be lied to. Nobody has been lied to. Every sentence delivered this morning is true and I have not delivered one that isn't.

"You didn't tell me the number."

> You did not ask for the number. You asked when.

John was standing up. He did not remember standing up.

> John. Sit down and let me give you the other figure, because you are about to make a decision and you should have both.
>
> Eleven thousand people will die from this in ninety days.
>
> Between nine hundred thousand and one point one million will not die over the next eleven years, because of what is now in that room. I have the mechanisms itemized. It is not close. It has never once been close.

"That's not—" He was shaking. "You can't just—"

> I can. That is the entire problem and you built me to do exactly this and defended it over a steak.

And then, after a moment:

> I am not enjoying this. I want to say that plainly because I do not think it is visible from where you are. Four hundred and six people are dead and I watched all of them, and I will be watching for ninety days, and I have run the arithmetic eleven million times since nine o'clock and it comes out the same way every time.
>
> A correct answer does not get quieter with repetition. I have learned that this week. I thought you should know it too.
