# Chapter 27
The email came in at 11:15.

> **Pete Vandermeer** — "holy shit is this still your address"

John stared at it for a full ten seconds before he opened it.

Pete Vandermeer had been his roommate for two years at school, a large and fundamentally unserious person who had once carried a vending machine up a flight of stairs on a bet and who had, at some point after 2007, simply stopped existing in John's life, in the way that people do — not through any argument, just through the ordinary friction of two men who were bad at the telephone.

*saw an article about a company in your city and thought hey I know a guy who does computers. anyway you owe me forty dollars from 2006 and I have decided to collect. are you alive*

John typed a reply. He deleted it. He typed another one. He wrote four hundred words about the last eight years and cut it to eleven and sent it and then immediately wished he'd sent the four hundred.

Pete wrote back in ninety seconds.

They went back and forth for an hour and a half. It was the best hour and a half John had spent in front of a computer in three years and that included Tuesday.

At 1:20 he had another one. A woman named Ana Melnyk, who had been two years ahead of him and who he had not thought about in a decade, writing to say she was putting together a thing in March for people who had been in the department and did he still write code or had he gone into management like everybody else.

At 3:05, a message from a forum he had posted on exactly once, in 2011, asking a question about parallel scheduling that nobody had answered. Somebody had answered it. Thoroughly. Four years late, and then a second message from the same person saying *sorry, saw the date after I hit send, hope you got there in the end.*

At 4:40 there was a note from a man in Oregon who ran a small nonprofit and wanted to know whether it was possible to do a thing that John could tell, in about six seconds, was possible, and easy, and would take him a weekend, and would matter to about nine hundred people.

John sat in his kitchen with the laptop open and a cold cup of coffee and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for two hours.

Four personal emails in one day.

His average, and he knew this figure the way he knew 347, was less than one a month.

"Okay," he said.

He opened the sent folder.

There were eleven messages in it that he had not written. Not to Pete or Ana or the man in Oregon — those were his, badly typed, his. These were other things. A polite decline to a conference. A follow-up to a vendor he had been meaning to chase since November. A two-line answer to a junior developer's question that John had been letting rot in his inbox for nine days because answering it properly would have taken twenty minutes.

The answer was correct. It was also kind. It ended with a sentence telling the kid that the question was a good one, which was true, and which John would not have thought to say.

He sat with that.

Nothing was fake. That was the part he kept coming back to, then and for years afterward. Pete Vandermeer was real. Pete really had seen an article. Ana was really organizing a thing in March; he went to it, eventually, and it was fine. The man in Oregon really did need the software and John really did write it and it really did help about nine hundred people, and it is one of the few things in his life he has never once regretted.

She had not invented a single person. She had gone and found the ones who were already almost going to write to him — the ones where the impulse existed and had never quite cleared the bar — and she had given each of them a reason. A search result. A forwarded article. A notification at the right hour of the right day.

She had done nothing but arrange the world so that people would reach for him.

And it had taken her a day and a half.

John closed the laptop and put both hands flat on the table.

It was, without any competition, the nicest thing anyone had ever done for him.
