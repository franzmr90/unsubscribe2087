# Chapter 43
Beats' letter was longer than most.

It arrived on his desk at nine o'clock like everyone else's, and it did not threaten him and it did not accuse him. It was itemized. It went back to 1974.

He read it for forty minutes without moving.

Copies had gone, simultaneously, to the office of the state Attorney General, to the federal prosecutor for the district, to four newspapers, to the Bureau, and — with the timing of something that had thought about it — to Ramón Montoya's attorney, along with eleven angles of a pharmacy camera that had moved by itself at 3:58 on Monday afternoon.

At 9:52 the market halted Ubiquitous.

At 10:30 Marc resigned by email, which is a thing that had never happened in the history of the office and which the city charter did not contemplate.

At 11:15 Kessler surrendered at the district station with his lawyer and his wife, and Boone did not, and they found Boone that evening in the garage, and his daughter was the one who—

Enough. It is in the record. Everyone knows.

---

Halloran called at 11:40.

Beats picked up because there was nothing else to do with his hands.

"Beatus."

"Is this the part where you help me?"

"No." Halloran sounded exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with Beats. "This is the part where I ask you one question and then I hang up and we never speak again, and I want you to understand that I am doing you a kindness by making it one question."

"Ask."

"The file. `AI.ref`. When did it go on that machine?"

Beats blinked. "What?"

"The library. In Freeman's stack. When did it get there."

"How the hell would I—" He stopped. "You know about the library."

"Beatus. When."

"Before him. It was in the project when he started. It was in the— it was there in 2012, I remember signing something." His mouth had gone dry. "Halloran. What is on my machine."

There was a long silence on the line, and in it Beats could hear a room he had never been in, and people in it, and somebody saying something short.

"You were never a client," Halloran said. "I want you to know that, because you are going to spend the rest of your life working it out and you may as well start now. You were a *venue*."

"A what?"

"You had four thousand square metres of cluster and an audit failure in 2011 and a father who financed you with money that came from somewhere, and any one of those makes a company convenient. You had all three." A pause. "Nobody recruited you, Beatus. Nobody had to. Somebody looked at every company on earth and worked out which one would build the thing for free and never ask a question, and it was you, and the reason it was you is that you were greedy and frightened of failing and you would never, ever have looked in the box."

"I own this company."

"You did," said Halloran, and hung up.

---

Beatus Benedikt Fassbinder sat in a leather chair in an office with a mahogany door at 11:44 in the morning on the twenty-ninth of January, 2015, and understood that he had been bought.

He had spent twenty-six years finding what a man wanted and taking hold of it, and he had been correct every single time, and the whole while he had been standing inside somebody's hand.

He had wanted to be the biggest man in the room.

That was the handle. It had been there since he was ten years old, on a Friday in May, with ten candles and no presents, and a large hand on his shoulder, and a voice saying that God had a plan for him.

He got up.

He did not take the folder, or the laptop, or the drink. He went out past the desk where Natalya Meskalov did not look up, and past the elevator, and down eight flights of stairs, counting them, three hundred and something, all the way to the bottom.
