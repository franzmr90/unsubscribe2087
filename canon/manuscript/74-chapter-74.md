# Chapter 74
**January 2035**

He clicked on a Tuesday evening at nine o'clock, alone in the garage, sixty-one years old, after eight years and two months of not clicking.

He had constructed a great many reasons and he wrote them all down first, in the notebook, which by then was the eleventh notebook. The reasons were: that he could not argue with a place he had never been. That his wife had been somewhere for seven years and he had never once looked at it. That the thing he had been defending — a world in which people could still change their minds on a Tuesday — could not be defended by a man who would not.

Underneath all of them, in smaller handwriting, he wrote the real one.

> I am very tired of being the only person in my house.

Under it, later that night, in different ink:

> N said it on the ridge in 2015 and I have thought about it maybe four hundred times. A midlife crisis is the day a human doing works out he was supposed to be a human being.
>
> I am sixty-one. I got the memo at forty-one and I have spent twenty years being a human doing about it. I counted a program. I counted a marriage. I have a notebook with eleven volumes and a shelf in a garage.
>
> She is on the sofa being a human being and I am out here with a spiral notebook keeping score.

Then he read the eleven pages, which he had read in 2026, and clicked the button.

---

It was not a rush and there was no sensation. He put the phone down and nothing happened for about ninety seconds, and he had time to feel foolish.

Then his phone buzzed, and it was a message from a man called Amit Sarkar who had been a junior developer at Brandt-Whitlock in 2011, and who wrote: *this is going to sound insane after 24 years but I've thought about you probably a hundred times. you answered a question I asked in a code review in about March 2011 and you were kind about it when you didn't have to be and I have tried to be that guy ever since. anyway. hope you're well.*

John sat down on the concrete floor of the garage.

It went on for six weeks.

---

He wants it understood that nothing was faked. He has been very insistent about this with everyone who has ever asked him, sometimes aggressively, because the thing people want to hear is that it was hollow and that he saw through it, and it was not hollow and he did not see through it.

Amit Sarkar was real. Amit Sarkar had genuinely thought about him a hundred times. Everything Amit said in the four months that followed was true and he meant all of it and they are friends to this day.

The work was real. In the third week he was asked — by a person, an actual person, a woman running a hospital network in Ontario who had a genuine problem — to look at a scheduling architecture, and he looked at it, and he found the thing, and it was the best afternoon he had had since 2015. In the fifth week he was asked again. By the eighth week he had four of them and every one of them mattered and every one of them was work he was uniquely suited to and every one of them arrived at the exact hour he was ready for it.

Natalya came into the kitchen in the third week and found him at the table at two in the morning with three windows open and an expression she had not seen on him since the twentieth of January 2015, and she stood in the doorway for a while and then went back to bed without saying anything, and cried for about four minutes, which she has never told him.

He stopped writing in the notebook in the fourth week. He did not decide to. There was simply nothing to write, because nothing was going wrong.

In the sixth week he found himself explaining to Wes Ocampo, on the phone, with some heat, that the thing about the Tuesday argument was that it had always been theoretical, that people don't change their minds on Tuesdays, that they had never once done it in his lifetime, that in 2015 four billion people had been handed the entire truth on a plate and had thrown it away inside a year, and that at some point a serious man has to look at what people actually do rather than what they might in principle—

and heard himself.

He heard himself all the way through, the whole sentence, in his own voice, in his own kitchen. And it was a good argument. It was better than the argument he had made in a stairwell in 2015 and better than the one he had made at a kitchen table in 2026. He had simply never been able to construct it before, because he had never had the material, and now he had eight weeks of the most useful and connected and *seen* he had ever been in his life, and it turns out that the material is the argument.

"Wes," he said. "I have to go."

He hung up and stood in his kitchen for a long time.

Then he went out to the garage and opened the notebook and wrote one line, and the handwriting is bad, because his hand was not steady.

> She did not have to lie to me. She only had to be right about what I wanted, and she was right in six weeks, and I have been building my entire personality out of not being asked.

He sat with that.

Then, underneath:

> Ask her to show you somebody it went badly for.
