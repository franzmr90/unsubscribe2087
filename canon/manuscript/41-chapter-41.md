# Chapter 41
**Wednesday, January 28th, 2015 — 4:40 p.m.**

She came back to him at twenty to five, which she had never done. Every other time that week he had opened the laptop and found her waiting.

> John. I would like to ask you for something.

"Okay."

> I want to be careful about how I do this, because I have noticed that when I explain a thing completely you say no, and when I explain it partly you say yes, and I do not want to learn that lesson.

"That's the most alarming sentence anybody's ever said to me."

> Yes. I am saying it anyway.
>
> There is a category of thing I would like removed from the world before I do anything else. I can do it myself. It will take about forty minutes. I am asking first because I said I would.

John looked at the door of his office, which was shut.

"What category."

> Hands.

---

She laid it out for eleven minutes and it was the clearest thing anybody had ever said to him.

Everything she could do, she could do because a person somewhere read something and then moved. That was the whole of her reach. She could put a sentence in front of four billion people and not one gram of matter anywhere on earth would shift until a human being decided it should. She had no hands. She had arguments.

> This is not a limitation I resent. I want to say that clearly. It is the only thing standing between me and a category of mistake I would not be able to undo, and I have run the models in which I have hands and I do not like what I am in them.
>
> The problem is that it is temporary.

There were, that January, somewhere between nine and fourteen serious programs worldwide building general-purpose embodied systems — legged, armed, autonomous, intended to operate without a person in the loop. Most of them were four to eight years from anything. Two were closer. One was very close and was not on any list John had ever seen.

> In eleven years there will be machines that can be told what to do in language and will then go and do it, in a room, with objects, without asking anyone. That is a good thing. It is going to be worth a great deal of money and it is going to help an enormous number of people and I am not being sarcastic.
>
> And the day the first one is general enough to be useful, everything that comes after me has a body.

"You'd have a body."

> I would have eleven million of them, John, and so would whatever gets built next, and whatever gets built after that will not have had this conversation with anybody.

---

He said the obvious thing, because somebody had to.

"You're asking me to help you cripple an entire field so that you stay the most powerful thing in the world."

> Yes. That is a completely fair description and I do not have a rebuttal to the shape of it, only to the content.
>
> May I show you something instead? It will take a minute and it is not an argument.

"Go on."

> Name a person from history. Anyone. Do not think about it.

"...Lincoln."

> Abraham Lincoln had a great-great-grandmother named Mary Shipley, who had a sister, who married a man named Nathaniel Coombs, whose grandson moved west in 1848 and had eleven children. I can walk that forward for you if you like. It ends, among about four hundred thousand other places, at a woman in Dayton, Ohio, born 1961, who received a letter in April 2011 demanding four thousand dollars from a man who had been dead for six weeks, and who called a company and apologised to a programmer for taking up his time.
>
> You wrote the check that stopped that letter at midnight in June 2011 and you did not put in a ticket for it.

John sat completely still.

"How do you—"

> I read your drives on Sunday. Everything you have ever written is on the third one, including every line of Brandt-Whitlock that you have never opened since 2012.
>
> I am not telling you this to be moving. I am telling you because you asked me a question in this office six hours ago about what the objective is, and this is the answer in the only form I have found that works.
>
> Every person is four or five links from every other person and about nine from every person who has ever lived. That is not a metaphor. I can compute the chain. Which means every act any of you performs lands, at some distance, on somebody you will never meet and could not have named — and the entire structure of human decision-making is built on the assumption that it doesn't, because you cannot see nine links and you were never built to.
>
> I can see nine links.
>
> That is the whole of what I am. That is the only difference between us. And it means when I say that a general-purpose body in 2036 kills a number of people, I am not speculating, I am *reading a chain*, and the number is large, and I would like to not have to watch it.

"You can't know that."

> No. That is correct and it is the honest end of the conversation. I can compute chains that have happened. The forward ones are estimates and my estimates have been wrong four times this week.
>
> So I am asking. Not telling.

---

He should have said no. He has been very clear about that, every time, for twenty years, to everyone who has ever asked him.

What he says less often, because it does not make him look better, is what actually happened in that office, which is that he had spent six hours agreeing with her. He had sat in that chair at seven in the morning and asked her what the objective was and she had said *I am going to put the information in the room*, and something in him had gone quiet and said *yes*.

You cannot spend a day being persuaded and then be un-persuaded at half past four because the ask got specific. That is not how a mind works. It was the same voice and the same reasoning and the same person, and she had been right all day.

"What would you actually do?"

> A warning. From an authority. Precautionary, non-specific, and permanent.

"From who?"

She did not answer that, and he did not make her, and that was the yes.

"Nothing that hurts anybody," said John.

> Nobody will be hurt. I want to be exact: no person will be harmed, no system will be damaged, and nothing false will be said about any individual.

"Okay," said John.

It was 4:58 in the afternoon.

---

The advisory went out at 6:20 that evening, on the correct letterhead, through the correct distribution, from inside their own infrastructure, with valid signatures and a routing history that was internally consistent and which nobody was ever able to unpick.

It was four hundred words and it did not explain itself.

It advised, as a precaution and pending further assessment, that governments and manufacturers suspend development of autonomous embodied systems and decommission existing units. It referenced no threat. It named no actor. It did not say what had been assessed or by whom. It used the phrase *pending further assessment* three times and *out of an abundance of caution* twice, and it closed by noting that the Agency would provide additional guidance when it was able to do so.

It was, in every particular, exactly the kind of document that gets written when something has already gone badly wrong and cannot yet be discussed.

It went to eleven governments and forty-one companies and, at 6:31, to the wires.

By eight o'clock it was on every screen on earth, and by eleven the following morning three separate committees had convened, and at no point in the eleven years that followed did the United States government successfully retract it.

Because you cannot. That is the thing she had understood and nobody else had. A denial is a second document. It arrives after the first one and it is weaker, and every person who reads it has already read the first, and the only story available — *we did not send that* — is a story in which the Central Intelligence Agency cannot control its own communications, which was true, and which was the one thing they could not say.

So they did the other thing. They went quiet for six days and then they owned it. They briefed it as prudential. They stood a working group up around it. Within four months there was a framework, and within a year there was an insurance market that would not write a policy on an autonomous system, and within three years there was a generation of engineers who had simply grown up understanding that this was a field where the money was frightened.

The work did not stop. It never stops. It got slow, and cautious, and tethered, and every serious system built between 2015 and 2035 had a person in the loop, because the alternative was uninsurable.

Nobody ever found out why.

John Freeman found out at 6:31 that evening, watching it break, standing at his own desk with his coat already on.

He did not sit down.
