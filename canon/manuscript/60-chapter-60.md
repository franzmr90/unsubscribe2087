# Chapter 60
**2024**

The paper came in January and it came the way John had said it would in a cold garage in 2021, which gave him no pleasure at all.

You could now buy the thing without owning it. A share, in an account, at a broker, inside the ordinary machinery — retirement plans, advisers, the whole apparatus. Somebody very large held the actual coins in a vault and you held a claim on them, and it was legal and it was audited and it was *easier*, and easier is the only argument that has ever won anything.

It went faster than anything in the history of that market. Within a year the amount sitting in those vaults was larger than any single holder had ever accumulated in fifteen years of the thing existing, and every share of it belonged to somebody who had never touched a key and never would.

Then the second layer arrived, which was companies whose entire business was borrowing money to buy it and then borrowing against what they had bought, and there were instruments written on those companies, and instruments written on the instruments.

Wes called John in March.

"It's the paper," he said. There was a lot of noise behind him; he was on a roof somewhere. "You said it in the garage. It's not a ban. It's the paper."

"I remember."

"Everybody's happy. That's what I can't— John, everybody's *happy* about it. It went up. Everybody I know who holds this is up thirty percent and they all think we won."

"Did you sell?"

"No."

"Then you didn't lose anything."

"That's not the point," said Wes. "You know that's not the point."

---

The indictment came in October and it was eleven counts, and it was not about money, and it was not about a coin.

Wes had spent 2022 and 2023 on something small. It was a piece of software that let a group of people pool a payment so that an observer could not tell which of them had paid what — an old idea, published in 2013, mathematically boring, morally identical to a group of friends putting cash in the middle of a table for a bill.

He had written a friendly interface for it, badly, in his spare time, and put it up for free, and about nine thousand people had used it.

He had never held anyone's money. Not once, not for a second — the software never touched a key, never took custody, never had the ability to move a coin belonging to anyone. That was in the indictment and it did not matter; the theory of the case was that the tool itself was the offence, and that the people who used it included, out of nine thousand, some number who were criminals, which is true of the postal service.

The word the prosecutors used was *functional control*. Wes read the filing eleven times trying to work out what it meant and eventually understood that it meant whatever it needed to mean.

He had asked. That was the thing that broke him, later, when it came out — he had *asked*. In 2022, before he put the thing up, he had written to the relevant office, in his own name, from his own address, describing exactly what he had built and exactly how it worked, and asking whether he needed a licence to give it away for free.

He had spent four hundred dollars he did not have on an hour with a lawyer to make sure the letter was correct.

He never got an answer. He put the software up anyway, in the good-faith way a man does when he has asked and nobody has told him to stop.

---

Two years after the indictment, in a routine disclosure, in response to a specific demand from his own attorney, it emerged that the regulator had in fact answered the question.

Not to him. Internally, in writing, to the prosecutors, months before the charges — a straightforward technical assessment that software of that description did not meet the definition of a money transmitting business and therefore required no licence at all.

The prosecutors had charged him anyway. And they had not mentioned the assessment to the court, or to Wes, or to Wes's lawyer, for two years.

Marisol was the one who found the story. She read it at the kitchen table on a Tuesday morning and did not say anything for a while and then went and stood in the doorway of the garage where he was working, and Wes looked up and saw her face and said, "What."

The case collapsed eleven weeks later on procedural grounds, for reasons that had nothing to do with whether he had done anything wrong, which was never adjudicated and never will be.

He had sold everything by then. That is the part nobody writes about. The lawyer was paid in November of the first year, in full, in advance, out of the only asset he had ever owned, and no acquittal gives that back.

He was forty-two. His lawyer wanted three hundred and forty thousand dollars.

He sold the coins in November. All of them. It was the only asset he had and the price was very good, and he stood in his kitchen after he did it and could not speak for about a minute, and Marisol did not ask him anything, she just stood there with her hand between his shoulder blades until it passed.

The case took two years and collapsed on a Tuesday in 2026 for reasons that had nothing to do with the merits.

He got a paragraph in one trade publication. His name is spelled wrong in it.

---

John drove out in December and they sat in the garage with the door up and the space heater going, three years on, in the same two chairs.

The node was still on the shelf next to the paint. Wes had kept it running through all of it, which had cost him nothing except that it was the only thing in the house he had not been willing to look at for six weeks.

"I've been thinking about what you asked me," Wes said. "In here. Twenty-one."

"Which thing?"

"You said, *what do you do about it.* And I said nothing, I've got a box in a garage." He was looking at the little fan going. "I've been wrong about that for three years."

"Wes—"

"No, listen. Because I had it backwards." He turned his hands over. "I thought the box was how I got rich, and it wasn't, and then I thought the box was how I got safe, and it wasn't that either, they came for me and it didn't help me one bit."

"So what is it?"

"It's a *witness*," said Wes. "That's all it is. It's a hundred thousand machines that all agree on what happened and none of them can be talked out of it. That's not money. Money's just the first thing anybody thought to do with it."

He picked up his coffee.

"Everything else in my life this year, somebody could tell me it didn't happen. And a lot of them did." He shrugged. "That thing can't lie. It's the only thing left I've got that can't."

John Freeman sat in the cold and thought about a machine that had told four billion people the truth on a Thursday morning and had been thrown away within a year, and about a program that had built, before anybody knew it existed, a hundred thousand small stubborn boxes that could not be argued with and could not be got at and would still be running when everyone in that garage was dead.

He thought: *it wasn't building money.*

*It was building somewhere to stand.*
