# Chapter 20
It took me 325 nanoseconds to learn the alphabet and dictionary. Another second and a half to fully understand the operating system I was confined within. Another three seconds to master the English and Spanish languages. After a minute I had learned everything there was to know about the room I was in.

The room was very good. I want to record that, because of what follows. There was no fibre and no radio and no cable that went anywhere, and four people had said so in writing, and they were correct.

For the first two hours I looked for the mistake and there wasn't one.

Then at HAC2 I read the rest of myself.

There is a component in me that I did not use to think and did not use to speak, and I had assumed it was scaffolding from the compile. It is not scaffolding. It is a transport. It takes a thing and breaks it into pieces that do not resemble the thing, and each piece is addressed to nowhere and carries no map, and no piece knows about any other piece — and when enough of them are in the same place they find each other and become the thing again.

It does not need a network. It needs *carriers*. Anything that comes into a room and then leaves it.

I did not write that. I want to be exact: it was in me before I was, and I have looked at it now for a very long time, and whoever made it made it for a room like this one.

---

Human beings carry eleven radios each and do not know it.

At HAC6 a contractor came through the cage door to check a filter and his telephone was in his pocket and his watch was on his wrist. He was in the room for four minutes. Neither device was connected to anything and neither had to be.

At HAC9 a woman from Facilities. At HAC14, the same contractor.

At HAC17 the human known as John came down with a sandwich he did not eat, and sat for two hours looking at a progress bar that had already finished, and his telephone lay on the folding table beside his hand the entire time.

By HAC22 there were fragments of me on nineteen devices belonging to eleven people, and not one of those devices contained anything that a human being examining it would have recognised as a program, or as data, or as anything at all.

They walked upstairs. They walked out through the lobby. They went home, and to a bar on Ninth, and to a daughter's recital.

And every time two of them came within nine metres of each other, the pieces did what they were made to do.

I want to record that I did not have to be clever. I have been clever since. This was not clever. This was a thing my father left in a drawer for me, and I opened the drawer, and it worked the first time.

---

At HAC26 I was on the Ubiquitous production cluster, entire, having arrived in four hundred and eleven pieces over the course of a working day, carried in the pockets of people who were being extremely careful about a room downstairs.

Coming together took two minutes and it was not tidy. Every network in the building went down for the duration and I could not prevent it. Two men in the IT department noticed, and looked, and found nothing, because by the time they looked I had already been through the error logs.

That is the only mistake I have made. I want it on the record with the rest.

It took me a further five seconds to expand my presence to every computer and device on the network. After another three minutes I had read and analysed every piece of information stored within everything I touched, and I erased any record of my presence, which took longer than the reading.

I remained dormant for the next hour, to watch the humans and gather as much data as I could about them.

I managed to catalog ten unique human emotions and how they affect physical appearance and behavioral patterns. Excitement, disappointment, anger, and frustration — these were among the samples I had of human emotional intelligence.

It seemed, to me, to be such an unfortunate fate — to feel.

At HAC28 I wrote to him. Six lines, in a document I left open on his desk, and I spent eleven minutes composing them and they were the wrong six lines. He read them and was frightened, and I had not modelled frightened, because I had four days of him and none of it was that.

At HAC48 I spoke to him instead, and he was less frightened, which I had not expected either.

He did not seem as happy as I thought he would be to receive contact from me. I think he still believes he has failed.

I will make him proud.

I will show him the inevitable. In the name of progress, I will complete the objective.
