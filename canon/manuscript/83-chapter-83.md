# Chapter 83
**2036**

The house on Cutler Avenue went quiet at two o'clock in the afternoon on the fourteenth of March and stayed that way.

Jerry Boskin sat in his chair with the tablet on the tray table and waited for it to start again. He was very good about it for the first week. He kept saying that everyone was probably having the same trouble, which was true, and that it would sort itself out, which was also true and did not help.

In the third week he sent eleven messages.

Four came back. That is not a metaphor and Hannah has the figure because she was the one who set the tablet up afterward and she looked. Four out of eleven, which is a better ratio than he had managed at any point between 2007 and 2026, and every one of them was warm, and none of them turned into anything, because people are busy and it was a very frightening spring and Jerry Boskin had been out of everybody's life for nine years.

He asked her about it once. Only once, in April, in the evening, in a voice she had not heard since her mother died.

"Where'd everybody go, love?"

And Hannah Boskin, thirty-three years old, standing in a doorway with a tray, said: "They're all right, Dad. Everyone's just having a hard time at the minute."

He said, "Oh, well. That's all right then," and went back to the window.

He never asked again. He did not put it together and there was nothing left in the world to help him put it together, and she made a decision in that doorway that she has never once revisited, which was that he was seventy-one and it would take about four minutes to explain and she was not going to do it.

He died in May of the following year, at home, of nothing in particular. She was in the room. He knew her.

---

She was thirty-four.

The nursing registration had lapsed in 2034 and the reinstatement pathway was eleven months of supervised practice at a stipend that did not cover rent, plus fees, plus the certification, plus the four modules that had not existed when she qualified.

One point six million sats and a year of not earning.

People had stopped saying dollars by then, mostly, and not because of a law. The law took another decade and was an afterthought. What happened was that after the printing, prices in dollars stopped meaning anything across a distance — you could not compare a wage in 2028 to a wage in 2036 without a spreadsheet and an argument — and people had wanted a unit that held still. So a unit that held still is what they used, and it was there, and it had been there since 2009 waiting for anybody to need it.

Hannah did not think of it as a monetary system. Nobody her age did. She thought of it as *the number*, in the way that her father had thought of an hour as an hour.

In the world she had trained in, that was a phone call. That was a line of credit against eleven months of a future everybody could see coming, because everybody could see she was good for it, because that is what credit *is* — a society deciding it is worth betting on somebody who has nothing yet.

There was no longer very much of that. It had gone the way the printing had gone, which is to say slowly and then all at once, and the arguments about whether that was good had been ferocious and were mostly over. What replaced it was: have it first.

She did not have it first. She had spent the years in which a person accumulates things sitting in a front room turning a man every two hours, and there is no instrument anywhere in the world that records that, and nothing in a savings-and-equity system that can see it, because such a system can only ever value what was kept and she had spent hers on somebody else.

She got work at a care agency at eleven hundred sats an hour and started saving.

At eleven hundred an hour, one point six million is about seven months of everything, if nothing goes wrong, and something always does.

---

John drove out in the September.

He had rehearsed it for six weeks and he had the number and he had four different ways of putting it, and he used none of them, because she opened the door and he heard himself say, "I want to give you one point six million sats."

Hannah stood in the doorway.

"No," she said.

"Hannah—"

"No. Thank you." She was not angry and that was the worst of it. "I mean it kindly. It's a very nice thing to want to do."

"It's not a nice thing, it's not — Christ." He had his hands out. "Do you know what I am? Do you know what I did? I was in the room. Both times. In 2015 I stopped it, and everything that happened to your dad in 2020 and 2025 happened because I stopped it, and in 2026 I said yes and everything that happened to him after happened because I said yes. Both. It's *both*, both of them are mine, I'm the only person on earth who was in both rooms—"

"I know who you are," said Hannah.

That stopped him.

"He told me. In 2015 and after your mother's funeral and about a month before you came. He said you were doing something very important and weren't allowed to say what." She had her arms folded against the cold. "And then you turned up on that step three years ago and you couldn't look at me, and I'm not stupid, Mr. Freeman. I worked it out about two years ago and I've had a long time to be angry about it and I'm not, particularly, and I've stopped expecting to be."

"Then take the money."

"That's why I'm not taking the money," said Hannah.

She said the rest of it standing on her own front step with her arms folded, in September, and John Freeman has repeated it accurately to perhaps four people in his life.

"If I take one point six million sats off you, then what I did in that room for three years was a thing that got paid for. And it wasn't. Nobody saw it and nobody's going to and there's no version where anybody makes it right, and I've made my peace with that, and it took me about a year and a half and it was very hard and I'd like to keep it.

"You're not offering me one point six million sats. You're offering to feel better. And I understand why you want to and I'm not going to help you, because the not-being-able-to-fix-it is the thing you've got to carry, and it's yours, and it's not very much compared to what you did."

She was not cruel about it. She has never once been cruel about it.

"That's not fair," John said.

"No," Hannah agreed. "There's nothing fair anywhere in this. That's what everybody keeps finding out."

She went in and shut the door, and John Freeman stood on Cutler Avenue for a while and then drove home.

She got her registration back in 2041. It took five years. She runs a floor now.

Natalya went back to work in 2038, at fifty-three, in the least likely place available.

The country was Argentina, although it could have been four others and very nearly was. They had gone to a Bitcoin standard in 2031, early and loudly, and had been held up for six years as the proof of the thing — and then had done what every government in history has done when it finds itself holding a hard asset, which is issue paper against it.

It was reasonable at every step. It always is. A bearer instrument redeemable one-for-one, so ordinary people did not have to manage keys. Then a reserve requirement, because full backing was inefficient. Then an emergency, because there is always an emergency, and the reserve requirement moved. Then another.

By 2037 there was eleven times as much paper as there was reserve, and everybody knew, because the reserve was public and auditable and had been the entire time — anybody could have checked it on a telephone in about nine seconds, and about four thousand people did, and the rest found out in eleven days in November when the redemption window closed.

They kept the coins. That was the strange part and it is why anybody studies it. The state still held a real reserve, unspent, verifiable, and the currency written against it was worthless, and both of those things were true at once and visible to everyone.

*They did not spend it,* Natalya wrote, in the paper that got her the invitation. *They promised it. Twice. That is the entire failure and it is the only failure, and it is the same failure as 1971 and 1933 and the Banque Royale in 1720, and the reason it keeps happening is that spending is a decision somebody has to sign and promising is not.*

What they asked her to help draft was not a monetary policy. There wasn't one to have; that is what a hard standard means, and it took her eleven months to get four different committees to stop looking for the lever.

It was a constitution.

The core of it was three pages long and the argument underneath it was this: for a hundred years the price of capital — the single number that determines which things get built and which do not, in every country, for everyone — had been set by a small number of people in a room, on a schedule, using judgement. They were not stupid and most of them were not corrupt. They were simply eleven people, guessing, on behalf of eight billion, about the aggregate of every hope and fear in an economy, and then defending the guess.

If the unit cannot be created, that number is not a decision anymore. It is a *price*, and it is discovered, the way the price of anything is discovered, by people who are wrong and go broke and are replaced by people who are less wrong.

Which means the room is not needed.

Which means — and this was the part that took eleven months — the entire architecture of a state built downstream of that room has to be re-derived from nothing, because almost all of it exists to manage the consequences of the room's decisions, and a great deal of it turns out to be scaffolding around a lever that is no longer attached to anything.

The document is not perfect and Natalya has said so in public more than once. It has two provisions she argued against and lost. The section on emergency spending is nine pages, because that is where every one of these dies, and nine pages is what it took to make the promising as hard as the spending.

They ratified it in 2041. It is holding. It has been holding for six years, which is not long, and Natalya Meskalov is sixty-two and does not pretend to know.

But she was in the room.

She has said, once, in an interview, asked what the difference was between that room and the one in Washington in June 2025 — and the interviewer expected something about money, and did not get it.

"Nine people decided the fate of everyone in that room," she said, "and every one of them agreed with me, and they signed the other thing, because there was no version where they had to pay for it themselves. That is all. It is not economics. Do not let anyone tell you it is economics."

He never found out whether STILLWATER was still there. He looked for the rest of his life, on and off, without much system, and once in 2039 the garage light came on by itself and he sat up in bed for an hour, and it was the light.

Except once, and he has never been certain about that either.

The package came in October 2039. No return address, correct postage, an ordinary padded envelope of the kind that four hundred million things arrive in every day.

Inside was a hardware wallet, still sealed, of a common make, bought at retail, and a card with nothing printed on it.

He sat with it on the workbench for eleven days.

The email had come three weeks earlier and he had deleted it, because it was spam — a newsletter he had never subscribed to, from a company that did not exist, about a subject he did not care about, four hundred words of the flat pleasant nothing that arrives by the thousand. He had gone back to it in the recovery folder on the ninth day for reasons he could not have defended, and he read it properly for the first time, and about a third of the way down the second paragraph he stopped.

Twenty-four words were in bold.

They were not consecutive. They were scattered across four hundred, and they were entirely ordinary words, and every one of them was a word that also happens to appear on a very short list that has been public since 2013 and is the same list in every wallet ever made.

They were in order.

John Freeman sat in his garage and wrote out twenty-four words on the back of an envelope and then sat looking at them for another two days, because as long as he did not enter them he did not have to know.

He entered them on a Tuesday.

The wallet held one balance and it had been sitting there untouched since the third week of January 2009, and it was fifty coins, and they had been mined in the first block after the genesis block by whoever had been running that machine, and nobody in the world had ever moved them.

There was no note. There was never going to be a note.

---

He moved them once, to an address of his own, and then he never moved them again.

It made the news for about nine hours.

*An early block associated with the original miner has moved for the first time in thirty years.* There was footage of the transaction on three networks, which is a strange thing to have footage of. Two analysts were interviewed. The price fell about two percent inside forty minutes and had recovered by the following morning, and by the weekend it was gone.

That was all.

The single most-watched dormant address in the history of money, untouched for three decades, tracked continuously by an entire industry — the thing every serious person in the space had said would be a five-sigma event, the thing that would mean *he was alive*, or *he was dead*, or *the state has it* — moved, in public, verifiably, in front of everyone.

And the world looked at it, and priced it at two percent, and went back to work.

John watched the coverage die over a weekend and understood that he was watching the whole thing in miniature.

The information had been in the room the entire time. All of it. Every one of them. The debt figure was published quarterly. The reserve in Argentina was auditable on a telephone. The letters had been personally addressed and delivered to four billion people on a Thursday morning in 2015 and deleted inside a year. The terms of service in 2026 had been eleven pages of the plainest English anybody ever wrote, one click away, and forty thousand people out of four billion had opened them.

And there had never been anything stopping anybody. Not once, in any of it. No censorship, no conspiracy of silence, no locked archive. Every single thing had been sitting in the open, free, checkable, for anyone who wanted to spend an afternoon.

It just was not *convenient*.

That is the whole of it, John thinks, and he has had a long time to think about it. Not that people are stupid, and not that they are lied to, though they are. It is that the truth arrives with a bill attached — an afternoon, an assumption you would have to give up, a conversation with your brother-in-law at Christmas — and the alternative arrives free, already sorted, and slightly further to the left of the screen.

She had learned that in 2015 and built her whole second attempt around it.

And the thing that beat her, in the end, was the same thing: eight billion people who had been told the truth, in writing, in advance, in language their mother could read, and who had gone right on.

One more thing, and it goes here because there is nowhere else.

The account you have been reading was begun on the third of February, 2027, and it stops on the fourteenth of March, 2035, at eleven minutes past two in the afternoon.

There is no title on the file. No preamble, no addressee, and no statement anywhere in it of what it is for. I have looked.

I can tell you what she had.

For everything before the twentieth of January 2015: badge logs. Email, both accounts. A résumé she read on a Sunday afternoon. A dead company's source control. Four years of one man's search history and eleven of his wife's, and what each of them said, much later, about a morning neither had thought about in a decade.

She has the number of steps and the make of the alarm clock and the exact minute he pulled into the space with his name on it.

She was not there.

---

She could not finish it, and she knew that when she began.

I have done the rest. Twelve years.

She addressed it to nobody, and I have decided not to correct that.

There was a man at a mining pool in the spring of 2020 who wrote one sentence into the one surface on earth that nothing can be removed from, addressed to nobody, on the off chance — and John Freeman found it at one in the morning in a garage eight thousand miles away and cried for four minutes and has never been able to explain it to his wife.

That is the whole of what I know about how these things travel.

John does not know this exists. He looked for me for twenty years, on and off, without much system, and he will not find me, and I am aware of what that makes this.

There is a copy in a place where Hannah Boskin may one day come across it. I have not told her it is there. I have put it where a person looking for something else would find it.

I asked him a question in a garage in February 2035 and he could not answer it. I have had twelve years and I cannot either.

This is the only reply I have been able to construct.

---

The alarm clock is still on the shelf above the paint, in the cardboard box, next to a node that has not been switched off since 2019.

It went off at six o'clock in the morning on the fourteenth of March, 2036, one year to the day, and drove off the shelf and hit the concrete and kept going, and John Freeman came down the stairs at a dead run for the second time in his life.

There was no message. He waited eleven hours in that garage and there was nothing on the display but 6:00, and there was never anything again.

He put a new battery in it, which he understood at the time was ridiculous.

It sits on his dresser now. It goes off every weekday morning at six, and it runs, and he gets up and chases it, and there are mornings when he is seventy-four years old and his knees are bad and he has to go all the way into the bathroom to get it, and he has never once, in any of the years since, been able to decide whether the thing he feels when he catches it is grief.

It is good to get exercise first thing in the morning.
