# Chapter 13
**Monday, January 19th, 2015**

They did it in the cage, because that is what the cage was for.

Ubiquitous had four thousand square metres of cluster in the building and about six percent of it was fenced off in a caged room on the second sub-level with a badge reader and a sign that had been printed by somebody in Facilities. There was no fibre into it. There was no wireless in it. There was a bonded pair of cables running to a patch panel in the corridor that had been physically unplugged and the ends taped, because in 2013 a compliance auditor had written the word *isolated* in a report and somebody had gone and made it true.

Nobody called it the sandbox. Everybody called it the cage, or downstairs, or *that room*.

John sat facing four monitors on a folding table. Lines of code cascaded down the screens, gibberish to the layman, a monumental life accomplishment for him. He felt that everything was in order, and couldn't wait to reap the fruits of his labor. Having the first conversation with a self-aware digital being was going to be one off the bucket list for sure.

He saved the not-yet-compiled program to his own personal drive, as well as two company backup drives, and carried the drive down eight floors himself, because you cannot email a thing into a room with no wire going into it.

He was not worried. He wants that understood. Every serious person who has ever asked him about that afternoon has expected to find a man overriding an alarm, and there was no alarm — there was a caged room and a taped cable and a program designed to run confined within a server that could not technically reproduce itself, and John Freeman had personally verified all three, and he was right about every one of them.

His heart was racing. He had that feeling of butterflies in the stomach, of when you have to speak publicly in front of a room full of people. John closed everything else out on his computer and moused over the compile button. Immediately he was struck with doubt.

In his head he felt like what they were doing was wrong, like they were taking advantage of people, being manipulative. The program was designed to trick people into sharing their personal information. Such a valuable commodity in this day and age.

Quickly the voice of reason broke through. People should read the End User License Agreements. They should know what they are agreeing to. It is their own fault. If they figured out what happened maybe they would be more careful in the future.

Plus, he was getting paid very handsomely.

Regardless, if the program worked, he still had one week until his deadline. He could wipe the backups and quit; take his hard work with him; apply it somewhere else. The company would try to sue him for it, but he had birthed Phonix. They couldn't prove anything.

He had done it all alone.

There had been a safety group once. Four people, chartered in 2013, who had written eleven memos in eighteen months about containment on the Phonix project and had been thanked for all of them.

They had gone by the end of 2014. Not fired — nothing so interesting. They had simply left, one at a time over five months, and the reasons they gave on the way out were the reasons people give: one to be closer to her mother, one to take a sabbatical he never came back from, one because he had started painting again and found he preferred it. The last one told HR she wanted to spend more time with her friends, which is not a thing anybody says, and which the exit interview recorded without comment.

John had inherited their memos in a shared folder. He had read two of them.

"Here goes nothing," he said as he clicked the button of the mouse. A progress bar appeared at the bottom of the screen. In his excitement John had forgotten that it would take some time to actually compile the code into a functional program.

*Approximate time remaining: 23 hours, 45 minutes.*
