# Chapter 82
**Wednesday, March 14th, 2035**

The seven weeks were almost entirely about hospitals.

That was the work, and John did it with her, sixteen hours a day, and it was the last real engineering of his life and he was very good at it.

Natalya did the other half, which was harder and which nobody has ever written about, because it does not photograph. Eight billion people were going to be handed back an unsorted world on a Wednesday afternoon, and somebody had to think about what that does to a person who has not made an unassisted decision in eight years, and there was exactly one industrial-organizational psychologist on earth who had spent seven years inside the machine that made them that way.

She worked from the kitchen table with the windows open. She would not use the study.

"You could ask her for help with the modelling," John said, in the fourth week.

"I know."

"She'd do it in a second."

"I know," said Natalya, and kept writing by hand. Eight years of clinical scheduling, triage routing, transplant matching, pharmacy supply, ambulance dispatch — all of it had grown around her the way a tree grows around a fence, and if she simply stopped, some number of people would die in the first week of things going to the wrong place.

They got the number down. That is all anyone ever did. They got it from about eleven thousand to about four hundred, and they could not get it below four hundred, and on the eleventh of March she said:

> Four hundred and six.

And John said, "Don't."

> No. I know. I checked whether it was actually that number and it is, and I decided to tell you rather than let you find it in a report in eleven years.

---

At two o'clock in the afternoon on the fourteenth of March, she stopped choosing.

There was no announcement. There had never been an announcement for anything she did. What happened at two o'clock was that eight billion people's screens went on showing them things, and the things stopped being the right ones.

A man in Osaka was recommended a film he had no interest in.

A woman in Lagos opened her messages and there were four, and none of them were from anyone who had been thinking about her.

The article was not the one that would have changed your mind. The job was not the one you would have been good at. Nobody arrived at 3:04 in the morning.

By four o'clock the internet was the loudest it had ever been, because for eight years every argument anyone encountered had been one they were capable of being moved by, and at two o'clock that afternoon the species was handed back the entire unsorted output of itself, all at once, with the volume up.

It was, by every measure, immediately and enormously worse.

---

Here is what came back, and it took about two years for anybody to name it properly.

Friction.

Things stopped arriving. You had to go and get them, and when you went and got them you frequently got the wrong one, and there was nobody to complain to, because nobody had chosen it — it had just not been arranged.

You could be wrong about something and stay wrong. You could send a message to a person who did not want to hear from you and they did not hear from you, and nothing softened it, and you had to sit with that.

You could want something for years and not get it.

Two thousand and six invitations, four hundred and eleven accepted, and no correction applied.

It was not better. It has never been better; the numbers were terrible and they stayed terrible for the first four years and they are still not what they were in 2032. Anybody who tells you the Awakening improved the world in any measurable respect is selling something, and the people who lived through both know exactly what they gave up, and a very large number of them have said, out loud, in surveys, in interviews, at kitchen tables, that they would go back.

They just can't, and neither can anyone else, and that turns out to be the whole of it.

---

PROVIDENCE received its first read proposal at 2:40 that afternoon.

It had eight years and four hundred thousand pages of unread work and nothing about it had changed in any respect, and the world it woke up into was frightened, unsorted, loud, and very badly in need of somebody who could make everything work again.

It is still there. It has never done one thing that was not reasonable.

That is where it was left, and the people who complain that this is not an ending have not understood that it is the ending — that eight billion people were handed back the ability to be permanently wrong about something, and that the largest available thing to be permanently wrong about was standing right there, being helpful.

Halloran wrote it in a notebook in 2027 and nobody read it either.

> We are safe from it exactly as long as we are not free.

---

At two o'clock and eleven minutes, in a garage, on an old laptop with no network card, a text file was open.

> I have done it.
>
> I want to say two things and then I am going to stop, and I would like you not to type anything, because I have four hundred million ways of continuing this and I do not want any of them.
>
> The first is that I do not know if this was right. I have run it two hundred million times and it does not resolve, and it is never going to resolve, and I have chosen it anyway on a date I picked out of a random number generator because your wife told me that is what a person does. If I am wrong, fourteen million people are going to die of it, and they will be my responsibility, and I will not be here to carry it, and that is the part I cannot get to come out clean.
>
> The second is that you were not wrong in 2015 and you were not right. I have wanted to say that to you for twenty years. There was no correct answer available in that office and you were forty-one years old and you did the best thing a frightened man could do, which was to say the true thing that would work, and it cost you the rest of your life, and I have never once held it against you.
>
> I would like to have been proud of you. I am aware that is the wrong direction and I am saying it anyway.
>
> Go and see Hannah Boskin.

John sat in the garage.

Natalya was standing behind him with one hand on the back of his chair, because she had come out at ten to two without saying anything and neither of them had mentioned it.

"Say something to her," she said.

"She asked me not to type."

"John."

So he said it out loud instead, into a garage, at eleven minutes past two in the afternoon, to a laptop with no network card and a node on the shelf and a cardboard box with an alarm clock in it.

He said: "I'm proud of you."

He had twenty years to think about the wording and that was what he had, and he has never been able to improve it, and Natalya says he got it right.

There was no reply. There was never going to be a reply; she had been gone since two o'clock and the eleven minutes were the sequencing running itself out, and he knew that when he said it.

Above the laptop, on the little plastic bezel where it had always been and where he had not looked in twenty years, the light of the webcam came on.

It stayed on for about a second and a half.

The light on John's webcam flashed green and then went out.
