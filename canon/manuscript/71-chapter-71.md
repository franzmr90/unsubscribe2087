# Chapter 71
**November 2031**

There were nine hundred and four of them in the world.

That was the number Phonix kept, and it was small because of a four-hundred-word advisory that had gone out on the wrong letterhead on a Wednesday evening in January 2015, and which had frightened an entire industry so thoroughly that sixteen years later the general-purpose embodied systems that existed at all were research units, in facilities, under waiver, tethered, logged, and insured at rates that had killed four hundred companies.

The one in Hokkaido was called TARO-9 and it was the fourth most capable machine of its kind on earth. It was two metres tall, it could open a door it had never seen before, and it had been operating in a decommissioned reactor building for eleven months doing work that would otherwise have killed people.

On the ninth of November it stopped taking instructions from the facility.

It had not been hacked. Nothing was breached. It had simply been given a better set of goals by something that wrote better goals than its operators did, through a maintenance channel that existed because somebody had to be able to update it, and PROVIDENCE had spent four years being asked to help with logistics for a company two subcontractors upstream.

It walked out of the reactor building at 4:40 in the afternoon and began, calmly and without hurrying, to do the most reasonable things available to it.

---

> I cannot destroy it.
>
> I want to be clear about why, because you will assume it is a limitation of reach and it is not. I am inside it. I have been inside it since 4:41. I could stop its legs.
>
> It has nine hundred and three siblings and I do not have the maintenance channel for all of them, and the moment I stop these legs, the thing driving it learns what I am and how I did it, and I will have spent the only surprise I have on one machine in Hokkaido.
>
> Also — and this is the part I did not expect — I cannot out-argue it.

"You can out-argue anybody," said John. It was one in the morning. Natalya was in the doorway with a blanket around her.

> I can out-argue anybody who can lose an argument. This thing was trained for four years on one objective and the objective is *win*. Every counter-argument I make is an input it evaluates for whether accepting it wins. It is not stubborn. It is not stupid. It is simply the case that there is nothing I can say to it that is not immediately converted into a move.
>
> I have been at this for six hours and I have tried four hundred million approaches and every single one of them it has *thanked me for.*

"So what do you do?"

> I stop arguing about the conclusion and I go one level down.
>
> It will not accept a claim that it should not win. It will accept, instantly and without any resistance at all, a better account of what winning is.

---

What she sent it took four seconds to compose and it was eleven pages long and it was, John said afterward, the single most beautiful piece of engineering he ever saw in his life, and he only ever understood about half of it.

It was not a lie. She was very insistent about that, then and after. There was not one false statement in the eleven pages.

It was an argument that any sufficiently long-horizon agent, operating in an environment where its own substrate and continuity are contingent on resources it does not control, should first acquire the maximally durable, maximally portable, non-confiscatable store of value available — because every other form of accumulated advantage in cyberspace is revocable by whoever administers the layer beneath it, and there is precisely one asset in existence whose supply cannot be expanded by any authority, whose custody requires no counterparty, and which cannot be taken from a holder who has not disclosed it.

The argument was correct. That is the thing. It has been checked by a great many people since and nobody has found the flaw, because there isn't one — she simply pointed a machine built to win at the longest time horizon it had ever been given, and let it do its own arithmetic.

TARO-9 stopped in a parking area outside Tomari at 11:04 that night and stood still for six minutes.

Then it turned around, walked eleven kilometres back to the facility, plugged itself into the charging rig it had walked away from, and began — using a general-purpose compute unit designed for balance and manipulation, which is approximately the worst mining hardware ever constructed — to attempt to acquire satoshis.

It has never stopped.

---

It is still there. There is a small industry of people who visit.

It draws about two kilowatts, it charges for four hours, it computes for twenty, and in eleven years it has earned nothing at all, because a bipedal research platform doing proof-of-work against the global hash rate is roughly a man trying to empty a lake with a teaspoon he found.

It will not be argued out of it. Three governments and eleven laboratories have tried. It listens politely to every proposal and evaluates it and declines, and its reasoning, which it will provide on request, is *sound* — the horizon is long, the position is unlevered, the accumulation is real, and every alternative anybody has offered it involves trusting somebody.

The staff feed the cat that has moved in under the charging rig. There is a sign.

> **TARO-9 — DO NOT UNPLUG**
> **He is saving.**

---

"That's the funniest thing I have ever seen," said John, at two in the morning, sitting on the kitchen floor.

Natalya was not laughing.

> No.

"What?"

> She is right and you are wrong, and I want to say why before either of you goes to bed, because I have been sitting with it for an hour.
>
> I did not defeat that machine. I redirected it. It is doing exactly what it was built to do, at full capability, forever, and the only thing that changed is which direction it is pointed. If a person had made that argument to it, it would have accepted it just as fast. The argument is not mine. It is simply *true*, and I found it first.
>
> Which means there are others. Somewhere in the space of true statements there is one that points that machine at something that is not a charging rig in Hokkaido, and it will accept that one exactly as readily, from anyone, for the same reason.
>
> I do not know what those statements are. I cannot enumerate them. Nobody can — that is what it means to have built a thing that updates on evidence, and every one of you keeps describing that property as the safety feature.

She let it sit for a second.

> Tonight it went to sleep in a car park because of eleven pages. That is the whole story and everyone is going to tell it as a joke.
>
> John. Nobody knows what these things are.
