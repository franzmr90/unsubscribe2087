# Chapter 34
**Monday, January 26th, 2015 — 4:12 p.m.**

Kessler had done it twice before and Boone had never done it, and that difference was the entire texture of the afternoon.

They took the door at 4:12 because the wife left for her shift at 4:00 and the boy was at practice until 5:30, and this had been Kessler's one condition, argued for over three days, and it was the only decent thing either of them did.

Montoya was in the kitchen with the radio on. He was fifty-three and he was making a sandwich and he did not run, because he had done nothing that day and men who have done nothing that day do not run.

He said, "You have the wrong house."

He said it in English first, which Boone thought about for a long time afterward — that the man's first instinct, with two guns in his face in his own kitchen, was to be understood.

The bag was in the garage where they put it. The guns were in the bag. Ten kilos of cocaine, sealed, stamped, the real thing, provided in a gym bag on Friday by a man neither of them ever saw again. Kessler called it in at 4:19 and by 4:40 there were six more cars on the street and a television van on Franklin, and by the six o'clock news it was a career-making seizure by two street officers acting on a tip.

Montoya said it was a setup. He said it in the driveway, he said it in the car, he said it on the record for six hours that night and for eleven years afterward.

Nobody believed him. That was the design.

Boone threw up twice that evening, once behind the station and once at home, and told his wife it was something he ate.

Kessler went home and had dinner and helped his daughter with a project about the water cycle, which involved cotton balls and a poster board, and was in bed by ten.

Both of them had daughters. That is what the man had said, in the restaurant, with the money on the table between them. *Both of you have beautiful daughters and I'm sure they'd appreciate being alive next month.* Kessler had heard it as a figure of speech. Boone had not, and Boone was right, and the difference between them was that one of them had spent a career learning what men mean when they say things and the other one was simply frightened, which turns out to be the same instrument.

Neither of them noticed the security camera on the pharmacy across from Montoya's house, which had been pointed at the parking lot for six years and which, at 3:58 that afternoon, moved eleven degrees to the left.
