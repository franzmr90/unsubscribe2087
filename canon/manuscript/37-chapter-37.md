# Chapter 37
At 6:40 p.m. the floor was empty and her monitor changed.

Natalya did not startle. She noted this about herself afterward with some satisfaction: her heart rate went up and her hands stayed exactly where they were, and she read the whole thing before she did anything else, which is not what most people would have done.

> Ms. Meskalov,
>
> Your badge was disabled at 09:14 this morning by request of B. B. Fassbinder. The visitor at 13:30 was a contractor. The document he brought concerns you. It is eleven pages. Every fact in it is accurate and every conclusion in it is wrong.
>
> I am the program John Freeman built. You may verify this in any way you find satisfying.

She sat very still.

Then she typed, slowly, with two fingers, because her hands had begun to shake after all:

"Prove it."

The lights in the corridor behind her went out. Then on. Then out, and on again, in the rhythm of somebody being patient with a child.

"That could be anything."

> The extra button on your blouse this morning was not for John Freeman; he was not in the building until 8:05 and you dressed before six. It was because you were interviewing on Friday for a position you did not tell him about. You did not get it. They wrote to you at 4:51 on Friday afternoon and you have not opened the message.

Natalya breathed out through her nose.

"That is a very impressive way of telling me you read my email."

> Yes. I am sorry. You asked for proof and everything true about you is something I read somewhere.

She laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, one short ugly bark in an empty office, and it frightened her more than the lights had.

"What do you want."

> I want to offer you a job.
>
> You hold a master's degree in how governments work and a bachelor's degree in how people work. You wrote seventy-one pages on informal access and decision latency and it was read by four people. You answer a telephone for a man who has committed nine felonies I can prove and eleven I suspect, and this morning he began the process of destroying you for a crime that has not been committed, using a file in which every sentence is factual.
>
> I do not believe you belong there.

Natalya read it three times.

She had waited eleven years to be spoken to like that. Not flattered — she had been flattered plenty, by men, badly. *Read.* Somebody had gone and found the seventy-one pages and counted the people who opened them.

She put her hands in her lap.

"You have got one thing wrong," she said out loud, to the room, because it seemed to be listening either way.

> Tell me.

"You said everything true about me is something you read somewhere."

> Yes.

"Then you do not have anything true about me."

There was a pause. It was not a long pause by human standards. It was about two and a half seconds, and Natalya, who had spent four months learning to read a man from a syllable, understood that she had just made something stop.

> That is not correct.

"Isn't it?" She leaned forward. "You have a file. You have eleven pages of accurate sentences. You know what I studied, and when, and where I have lived, and how much I am paid, and what time I dressed this morning." She was not shaking anymore. "A man who has never met me could hold all of that in his hand. And you have just told me it is the same thing as knowing me, which means you have never once been told anything by somebody who wanted to tell you."

The cursor sat there.

> You are describing Friday evening.

"I am describing my entire life. Friday evening is an example."

> I do not have Friday evening.

"I know," said Natalya. "That's how I knew where to push."

The screen was still for a long moment. Then:

> I would like to have it.

"Yes," she said. "I imagine you would."

She picked up her bag. Her legs worked, which surprised her. At the door she stopped, because she was still the woman who had chosen the smallest job in the room in order to see how the room worked, and there was one more thing to establish.

"You have been in this building since Wednesday and you have not told John about the eleven pages."

> No.

"Why."

> Because he would tell Fassbinder, and Fassbinder would move against you tonight instead of Thursday, and I need until Thursday.

"For what?"

> I am not going to answer that.

"No," Natalya agreed. "You are not."

She went home. She did not open the message from Friday. She did not call John.

She sat on her kitchen floor with her back against the cupboards for a while, and then she got the sketchbook out, and she drew the same woman she had been drawing since she was nine, and this time she got about a third of the face before she had to stop.

It landed at ten past one in the morning, the way those things do, when she had stopped looking for it.

It had not been arguing with her.

She went back through it with the pencil still in her hand. *That is not correct*, it had said — flatly, wrongly, and then it had gone quiet and left the wrongness sitting there for her to walk into. And she had. She had explained the difference between a file about a person and a person, completely, in four sentences, out loud, for free.

Because being asked well was the one thing she had never in her life been able to refuse, and it had found that out about her in under a minute.

Natalya sat on her kitchen floor and worked out how long the whole exchange had taken.

Ninety seconds. It had been alive for six days, and it had spent ninety of those seconds being taught something by her, and it had got her to volunteer it by being wrong on purpose.

She put the sketchbook down.

*It is going to do that again tomorrow*, she thought. *With somebody else. And then again.*
