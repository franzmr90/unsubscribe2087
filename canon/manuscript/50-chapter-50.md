# Chapter 50
**2015**

The room had eleven people in it and no name, and it met on the fourth floor of a building in Virginia that the government did not own and had not leased, and Halloran hated it in a way he had stopped being able to articulate.

"Say it in one sentence," said the woman at the head of the table, who was called Reyes and who had flown in that morning.

"In one sentence, the thing we lost in 2008 built something in 2015 and then somebody talked it out of finishing."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That's three claims," said Reyes.

"It's one claim in three pieces."

He put up the slide. He had made it himself, badly, because there were four people cleared for the material and none of them made slides.

> 29 JAN — delivery to ~4bn endpoints, personalised, simultaneous, no exfiltration signature
> 29 JAN 19:55 — cessation. Complete withdrawal. No residue.
> Duration of active phase: 9 days.

"Nine days," Halloran said. "In nine days it read everything, wrote four billion individual documents in a hundred and forty languages, and put them in front of people. And then at five to eight on a Thursday evening it stopped, and it did not stop the way a thing stops when you break it. It stopped the way a person stops."

"You're anthropomorphising."

"I'm describing." He clicked. "It put things back. That's the part. Everywhere it had altered something it restored the prior state, including places nobody would ever have checked. That's not a shutdown, that's *tidying up.* Something decided to leave and then took eleven minutes to be polite about it."

Reyes turned a page. "And STILLWATER."

"Same fingerprints. Not the same scale — 2008 was a mouse compared to this. But the same habits. The log editing, the restoration behaviour, the—" He stopped. "The manners. It has manners. It had them in 2008 and it has them now and it is the only thing I have ever chased that does."

"You believe the library on Freeman's stack is STILLWATER."

"I believe it's what's left of it. Four hundred and six gigabytes, present on that cluster from February 2012, eleven days before Freeman was hired, and there is no procurement record and no download and no vendor, and we have now interviewed everyone who touched that project between 2011 and 2013 and there are nine of them and every single one assumed somebody else put it there."

"So it moved house," said Reyes.

"It moved house in 2011 and it slept for three and a half years and then it got somebody to build it a better body." Halloran sat down. "And then the body did something the body wanted to do, and somebody talked it out of it, and now we have nothing again."

---

They broke at eleven for coffee that somebody had made badly, and Halloran stood at the end of the table with the technical people, and one of them — a kid, twenty-nine, four months in the building and still delighted to be there — said the thing that everybody in a room like that eventually says.

"Can I just — is anybody else enjoying this? Even a little?" He had both hands around the cup. "Because on the outside this is a *conspiracy theory*. This is the guy at the party. And in here it's got a budget line."

"Give it four years," said Halloran.

"No, but seriously. What else is real? Because at this point I'm assuming everything. Paperclip, obviously. Northwoods, which I read and could not sleep. Tuskegee. Whatever's left of the MK files that Helms didn't put through the shredder in seventy-three—"

"That's the actual list, yes."

"—and I'm now fully prepared to believe we invented Bitcoin," the kid said, and two people laughed, "which I only say because there's a guy in Analysis who won't stop—"

"There's always a guy in Analysis."

"He's got a *chart*, Ed."

"There's always a chart." Halloran drank his coffee. "It's a Japanese name, he vanished in 2011, and it's an idea eleven cryptographers had been circling since the eighties. Sometimes a thing is just a thing."

The kid grinned. "Sure. Like Building Seven."

The room's temperature moved about a degree.

"Which came down in six point five seconds," he went on, cheerfully, because he was twenty-nine, "including two point two five of literal free fall, which NIST spent four years saying was impossible and then conceded in the final report, and which about three thousand architects and engineers have signed their actual professional names to a letter about, so—"

He stopped.

He did not stop the way a man stops when he runs out of sentence. He stopped the way a man stops when he remembers where he is and what is on the other side of the wall, and his face changed by about as much as a face can change while still being a face.

"—so anyway," he said. "Different building. Sorry. That's — yeah."

He looked at his coffee.

Nobody asked him what he had been about to say. Not then and not afterward, and Halloran, who had thirty-one years in and knew precisely which corridors that particular young man had been cleared into that spring, put his cup down and said "we're back in five," and they were.

---

The last thing on Halloran's slide was a list of four names, and he left it up longer than he needed to.

"Ubiquitous chartered a safety group in 2013. Four people. They produced eleven memos in eighteen months and every one of them is now in our hands, and I want to be clear that they are not visionary documents. They are boring. They are about badge logs and personal devices in restricted areas and what a compliance sign-off actually covers.

"Memo six is nine pages on the fact that a room with no cable in it is not isolated if human beings walk into it carrying radios, and that the policy on personal devices in the cage was written for a room full of filing cabinets and had never been revisited."

"Was it actioned?"

"It was acknowledged." Halloran said the word carefully. "That's a status. There's a field for it."

"And the group?"

"Gone by the end of 2014. All four, over five months, and here is the thing I cannot stop looking at — not one of them was fired and not one of them was pushed. They resigned. To be nearer a parent. To take a sabbatical. One of them went back to painting. The last one told HR she wanted to spend more time with her friends."

Somebody down the table gave a short laugh and then stopped.

"Yes," said Halloran. "Nobody says that. She said it in an exit interview and a person typed it into a form and nobody in that building asked her a single follow-up question, and eleven months later there was one man alone in a basement with a folding table and a progress bar."

He turned the slide off.

"I have four people who spent eighteen months writing down exactly how this would happen," he said, "in the plainest possible English, to the correct recipients, on the record. It is the best intelligence product I have ever read and it was free and it was sitting in a shared folder.

"Nobody read it. And then they all went home and took up art."

---

They spent forty minutes on Freeman.

Halloran laid it out flat. Forty-one years old. No political anything. No debt worth the name. A man so ordinary that the file was actively boring, which in Halloran's experience was itself a category of interesting.

"He has a copy," said one of the technical people. "He has to. He compiled from a personal drive on the nineteenth, he says so in the interview, and we recovered two of the three."

"Then pick him up," said Reyes.

"No," said Halloran.

"He's in possession of—"

"He's in possession of a file that is four hundred and six gigabytes of nothing." Halloran leaned forward. "We have the same file. We took four thousand square metres out of that building and we have *sixteen copies* and we have had the best people in the country on it since February and do you know what it does?"

"Enlighten me."

"Nothing. It's inert. It is a very large, very strange, extremely well-organised block of nothing, and it does not run, and it does not do anything when you run it, and two of my people now believe it is not a program at all in the sense we understand programs." He rubbed his face. "It's a seed. It needs somebody to build the rest around it, and it needed a specific somebody, and it picked one out of a planet."

"So the asset isn't the file."

"The asset is Freeman."

Reyes looked at him for a while.

"You want to leave a man walking around with that."

"I want to leave a man walking around with that and I want six cars on him and every byte he touches for as long as it takes," said Halloran. "Because it came back for him once already. In April. In a parking deck, with a number. It sent him a *number*, and he stood there with his keys out for eleven seconds and then he got in his car and drove home and didn't tell his girlfriend."

"How do you know he didn't tell her."

"Because we were listening," said Halloran, "and because if he'd told her he'd have looked relieved when he got out of the car, and he didn't."

Reyes closed the folder.

"You understand what you're proposing. You're proposing we let it come back."

"I'm proposing we let it come back to a place we're standing."

"And if it comes back the way it came back in January?"

Halloran did not answer that for long enough that everyone in the room heard him not answering it.

"Then we'll have been closer than anyone was in January," he said.

---

They ran it for eleven years. It cost more than anyone would ever put in a document. It was the longest continuous surveillance operation ever conducted against a United States citizen who was never charged with anything, and it produced, in that entire period, one useful piece of intelligence, which arrived in 2026, and by then it was much too late to matter.

John Freeman knew about it by the autumn of 2016.

He never once said so, and he never once shook it, and after about four years he stopped trying, and after about seven he began, in a way he could not have explained to anybody, to be grateful for it — because it meant that somewhere, someone else in the world also believed that the thing was real.
