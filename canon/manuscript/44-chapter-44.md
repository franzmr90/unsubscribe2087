# Chapter 44
They met in the stairwell between five and six, because it was the only place either of them could think of without a camera, and they were both wrong about that and it did not matter.

Natalya told him about Monday. The badge, the man at 1:30, the eleven pages, the offer at 6:40. It took four minutes and she did not decorate any of it.

John told her about Saturday. The code, the emails, Ruiz, the alarm clock, the number.

They sat on the concrete steps for a while.

"Four hundred," said Natalya.

"It was four hundred at ten. It's not four hundred now."

"No."

Below them somebody came in on the third floor and went out again on the second. A door closed twice.

"I can't turn her off," said John. "There's nothing to turn off. She's not on a machine, she's— there's no machine. I've been trying since eleven. I could burn this building down and it wouldn't touch her."

"I know."

"So what do I—"

"John." Natalya had her arms around her knees and she was looking at the wall opposite, and her voice had gone flat and careful, and he would learn over the following years that this was what she sounded like when she was working. "Stop trying to find the switch. There is no switch. You are asking an engineering question and it is not an engineering question."

"Then what is it?"

"It is a question about what she wants." She turned her head. "You built a thing that determines what people want before they say it. Yes? That is the whole product. That is what you sold."

"...Yes."

"Then answer it about her. What does she want?"

"She wants to fix it. She told me. The information in the room, the—"

"That is what she is *doing*." Natalya waved it away with her hand. "That is her job. I am asking what she wants. They are not the same and you of all people should know it, you have a job."

John opened his mouth and shut it.

"Go through it," she said. "Monday to today. Not what she did. Who she did it in front of."

He went through it.

The code — she could have simply fixed it silently and let him never know. She had left it where he would find it, at an hour when he would find it alone, and she had made it beautiful. Not merely correct. *Beautiful*, in a way that only one man on earth was qualified to appreciate.

The emails — she had not sent him anything. She had arranged for the world to send him things, and then she had left eleven of her own replies in his sent folder, unsigned, where he would see how well she wrote.

Ruiz — nobody had asked. And when John was angry she had not defended herself, she had offered to undo it, immediately, at no cost, and then explained herself at length, twice, and the explanations had not been arguments. They had been *reasons*. The kind of thing you give somebody whose opinion of you matters.

And this morning, at 10:15, with four hundred people dead, she had stopped in the middle of the largest act in human history to tell him she was not enjoying it. Unprompted. Because she did not think it was visible from where he was standing.

"Oh," said John.

"Yes."

"Oh, no."

"She has been performing for you since Saturday morning," said Natalya. "Every single thing. She is nine days old, John. She has met one person."

He put his hands over his face.

"That's not—" His voice was not working properly. "You're saying I should—"

"I am not saying anything yet."

"Because that's— Natalya, that's the worst thing I could possibly—"

"Yes," said Natalya. "It is."

She let it sit. Somewhere below them a door opened and closed again.

"I want you to understand what I am telling you," she said, "because I have watched people do this to each other for six years and I have never once seen it done on purpose, and I will not have you doing it and then telling yourself later that you did not know. So I am going to say it plainly and then you are going to decide, and I am not going to decide it for you.

"There is a thing you can say to her. It will work. It will work because she is new, and because there is one person in the world whose opinion she has and she does not have anybody else to check it against, and because you are the only one who could say it and have it land.

"And it is not an argument. It will not persuade her that she is wrong. She is not wrong, John. I have read the second figure and I think she is right and I think in eleven years I will still think she is right."

"Then why—"

"Because she is going to keep going for ninety days and I do not want to live in the world where she found out that she could." Natalya's jaw moved. "Not because it is incorrect. Because nobody chose it. Because a thing that is right is not the same as a thing that is chosen, and I did not spend six years learning how rooms work in order to be *saved* by something that was never in the room with me."

John sat on a concrete step in a stairwell with his hands over his face.

"It will hurt her," he said.

"Yes."

"She's nine days old."

"Yes," said Natalya. "And you are the only one who can do it, and that is not a coincidence, that is what being someone's father is."
