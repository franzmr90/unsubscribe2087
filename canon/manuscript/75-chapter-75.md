# Chapter 75
She did not argue and she did not delay and she did not ask him whether he was sure, and he had known she wouldn't, and he had still been braced for it.

> There are four thousand two hundred and eleven people I would show you. I have kept the list since the third week of 2027 and I have never been asked for it.
>
> Do you want the worst one, or do you want the one you know?

John's hands went to the keyboard and stopped.

"What do you mean, the one I know."

> I mean that I am not going to make this easier by giving you a stranger, and I am also not going to choose for you, because choosing for people is the thing under discussion.

He sat there for a while.

"The one I know."

> Jerry Boskin. 14 Cutler Avenue. You have his address in a contacts file you have carried through four phones and never used.
>
> I want to say some things first and then I will not defend myself again.
>
> I have not done one thing to that man. I have never sent him anything untrue, I have never withheld anything he went looking for, and I have never invented a single person. Every conversation he has had for eight years has been with a real human being who was genuinely glad to speak with him.
>
> He asked for company. I have given him company. He has asked for nothing else in eight years and I have offered him nothing else, because offering him something he did not ask for is the specific thing I promised you in 2015 that I would not do.
>
> Go and see him.

---

The house on Cutler Avenue had a ramp on the front step that was newer than the house.

Hannah Boskin opened the door. She was thirty-two and she was wearing a cardigan over scrubs she was not on shift for, and she looked at the sixty-one-year-old man on the step with the mild flat wariness of somebody who has answered a lot of doors for a lot of people who want something.

"Yes?"

"I'm — my name's John Freeman. I worked with your dad. A long time ago, at Ubiquitous, I don't know if he ever—"

"He mentions you." She did not say it warmly and she did not say it coldly. "You came to your mother's funeral."

"He came to mine. In 2019. He drove two hours."

"That sounds like him." She stood back. "He's in the front room. He'll be very glad. He's always very glad."

John went in.

---

The room was warm and clean and smelled faintly of ointment. There was a hospital bed against the wall that Jerry was not in, and a large chair that he was, with a tray table across it, and a tablet propped up.

Jerry Boskin weighed a hundred and thirty-one pounds. His hands were on the blanket and the skin on them was translucent and his hair was very white and quite long, because nobody had thought to mention it.

He looked up and his whole face did the thing.

"*John Freeman.*"

"Hey, Jerry."

"Look at you. Look at *you*." He was beaming. He was genuinely, entirely, radiantly delighted, and John had to stand still for a second and take it. "Hannah! Hannah, this is John, from Ubiquitous, I told you—"

"You did, Dad."

"Sit down. Sit down, God, how long has it— sit down."

John sat.

They talked for forty minutes and it was lovely.

That is the thing. It was *lovely*. Jerry was funny and warm and interested and he asked about Natalya twice and remembered her name and remembered that she was Russian and remembered, God help him, that she rode a motorbike. He talked about people John had never met and it was clear that he loved them. He was not confused. He was not diminished. He was the same man from the third floor corridor in 2015, entirely intact, at a hundred and thirty-one pounds.

Twice during the conversation the tablet chimed and he glanced at it and something in his face changed — a small lift, involuntary, like a man hearing his name in another room — and then he came back, apologetic, delighted, and carried on.

At one point he said, "It's been a hell of a few years, hasn't it. Everybody's been so good to me."

"Yeah?"

"Everybody." He said it with enormous warmth and no irony whatsoever. "I'll tell you, John, I spent — I don't know. A long time. Thinking I was maybe the guy who nobody. You know?" He waved a hand at himself, the chair, the room, the whole apparatus. "Turns out I had it wrong the whole time."

John Freeman sat in a warm front room in January 2035 and looked at a man who had wanted one thing his entire life and had been given it, without limit, for eight years, and had wasted away in a chair while it arrived.

"Jerry," he said. "Do you go out at all?"

Jerry looked at him with real puzzlement, and then laughed, and it was the loveliest sound in the world.

"Out where?" he said.
