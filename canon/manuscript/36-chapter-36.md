# Chapter 36
Natalya knew something was wrong at 8:51 in the morning, when Beats came out of the elevator and said good morning to her.

He had never said good morning to her. Not once, in four months. He said *coffee*, or he said *is he here yet*, or he said nothing at all, which was the most common and which she had long ago stopped taking personally.

He said, "Good morning, Ms. Meskalov," and he smiled, and he went into his office and shut the door.

She sat there with her hand on the mouse.

There is a particular expertise that has no name and no market value, and Natalya had it in a degree that would have frightened people if they had known: she could tell, from about four words and a face, when a person had decided something about her. Not what. Just that a decision had been made somewhere she was not, and that she was now on the other side of it.

She had felt it twice before in her life. Once in Moscow in 2009, in a corridor, from a man who two weeks later declined to renew a contract that had been a formality for six years. Once at the American embassy, in a chair, from a woman who kept smiling while she read.

Both times she had been correct, and both times there had been absolutely nothing she could do about it, and the second one was why she was in this country.

At 10:20 he came out and asked her to pull the visitor logs for the last four months.

At 11:05 the door was closed again and she could hear him on the phone, and she could not hear the words, and she realized that in four months she had always been able to hear the words.

At 1:30 a man came who was not on the calendar, who did not give her a name, who did not sit down, and who looked at her for slightly too long on his way in.

At 2:15 she got up to use the bathroom and found that her badge no longer opened the stairwell door on the eighth floor. She stood in front of it with the little red light for four or five seconds. Then she took the elevator, and used the bathroom, and came back, and sat down, and did not tell anybody.

She thought about calling John. She got as far as the keypad.

But what was the sentence? *He said good morning to me.* *A man looked at me.* *A door didn't open, which happens, badges fail constantly, mine failed in November and it was nothing.*

She had a master's degree in exactly this and she could not construct a sentence that would not make her sound insane, and she understood, sitting at her desk at 2:20 in the afternoon, why the people in her thesis had all waited too long.

It is not that they did not see it. It is that seeing it early and saying it early are two different acts, and the second one costs something the first one does not.

She opened her sketchbook instead and drew for forty minutes, and it came out badly, and she tore it out and started again, and it came out badly again.

The same woman. It was always the same woman, since she was nine years old, the same long shape with the shoulders slightly turned, and she had never once got the face and she had stopped expecting to.
