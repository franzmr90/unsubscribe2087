# Chapter 31
**Thursday, January 22nd, 2015 (evening)**

Beats did not go home.

He drove for ninety minutes on the interstate at eleven miles over the limit, which was as close to meditation as he had ever come, and somewhere around the second interchange the rage burned down into something more useful.

He had been threatened perhaps forty times in his life. Most of it was noise. Twice it had been real, and both times the man had wanted something specific, and both times Beats had established what the man's actual price was inside of a week and paid it, and both men were still alive and one of them sent a card at Christmas.

This one wanted him to give away his company.

That was not a demand. Beats knew what a demand looked like; a demand had a number attached to it. This was a *sentence*, handed down, by somebody who thought they had the standing to hand one down, and the fact that they had not asked for money meant one of two things. Either they were a lunatic — and lunatics do not know your father financed the company, and they do not know about Montoya, and they do not use the word *narcotics* correctly in a paragraph — or they were somebody who already had everything money buys.

That was a much shorter list.

At 9:40 that night he pulled into a rest area outside the county line, took a phone out of the glovebox that had never had a call made on it, and dialed a number he had memorized in 2009 and never written down.

It rang four times.

"It's late," said Halloran.

"You're awake."

"I'm always awake. That's not the same as it not being late." A pause with something in the background, a television, a dishwasher, the ordinary furniture of a man's evening. "How's the ad business, Beatus."

"Somebody got into my mail."

"People get into everybody's mail."

"They sent it from inside. It came in, I read it, and while I was looking at it, it went away." He had not said this out loud yet and it sounded worse than it had in his head. "Off the screen. From the server, from the archive, from everywhere. And I have very good people."

Halloran did not say anything for slightly too long.

"You still there?"

"I'm here." The background noise had stopped. "What did it say?"

"That's my business."

"Beatus."

"It said give away the company."

"To who?"

"To nobody. To the *employees*." Beats heard his own voice climb and pulled it down. "Fifty-four percent, distributed. And resign. Three hours."

"And you didn't."

"Of course I didn't."

"And it's been—" A small sound, a man checking a clock. "About eleven hours. And nothing has happened to you."

Beats had not thought of it that way. He did not enjoy thinking of it that way.

"I want a trace," he said. "I want to know who is in my building. I want the name and I want it this week and I don't care what it costs, and before you tell me about warrants, I would remind you—"

"I know what you'd remind me of." Halloran's voice had changed. It had gotten friendlier, which Beats registered as compliance and which was in fact the opposite. "Don't do anything for a couple of days. Don't announce anything, don't fire anybody, don't move money."

"I don't take instructions."

"It's not an instruction, it's a favor, and I'm doing you the favor." A pause. "Send me the headers."

"The message is gone."

"Send me whatever's left of the message. And Beatus — the AI thing. Your Project Whatever. Where is it right now, physically."

"In a cage on the second sub-level. No fibre, no radio, and the one cable that goes anywhere has been unplugged and taped since 2013." Beats had said the sentence perhaps forty times in two years to perhaps forty different people and had never once been asked to prove it. "I paid for that room."

"Who has access?"

"Freeman. He built it."

"Alone?"

"Alone. That's the whole point of him."

"Huh," said Halloran, and Beats heard the noise of a man writing something down and mistook it, entirely, for a man being helpful.

"Two days," Halloran said. "Sit still. I'll call you."

He hung up before Beats could tell him he would not be sitting still, which meant that for the first time in twenty-six years, Beats had been the one left holding a dead phone, and he sat in a rest area outside the county line for some time turning it over in his hand and feeling, without being able to name it, that a conversation he thought he had won had gone in a direction he had not chosen.
