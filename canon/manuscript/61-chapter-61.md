# Chapter 61
**2025**

The number that broke it was not the debt. Everybody had stopped being able to see the debt years ago; it had passed the point where a figure means anything and become a kind of weather.

The number that broke it was the interest.

It went past a trillion dollars a year and then it went past what the country spent on its military, and it did this without any legislation, without a vote, without a single person choosing it, because compound interest is not a policy. It is arithmetic that has been running in the background since before anyone in the room was born, and it does not care.

Natalya was forty and had a title now, and she was in the briefing on the eleventh of June because her shop had done the collateral work, and there were nine people in the room and one of them was Carol Wendt, who was seventy-one and had come out of retirement for reasons she declined to give.

The slide was one line.

> **The obligation cannot be paid, defaulted, or inflated away on any timeline that does not destroy the currency.**

"That's not new," said Wendt.

"No."

"So why am I in Washington."

The presenter was a man of about fifty with the careful diction of somebody who has practised. He put up the second slide, which was the framework, and it took Natalya about four seconds to understand it and eleven years of training not to say anything.

The idea was elegant and she hated that about it.

If you cannot pay a debt and cannot default on it, you must find somewhere for the excess to go that does not raise the price of food. Every previous attempt in history had failed for the same reason: the money went into things people need, and then people cannot afford them, and then you have a country that is angry in a way that ends governments.

Unless there is a thing that is not needed by anybody. A thing with no industrial use and no consumer price, that can absorb an arbitrary quantity of purchasing power and simply *become more expensive* without a single household noticing.

A sink.

There is exactly one asset in the world with that shape and it was invented in 2008 by somebody nobody has ever identified.

"You want to inflate into it," said Wendt.

"We want to *route* into it."

"Say inflate, it's just us."

The presenter did not say inflate. He said the rest of it: reserve holdings, custodial concentration, the instruments that already existed and the ones that would be written, the collateral treatment, the twenty-year glidepath. It was, Natalya thought, the most careful piece of work she had seen in nine years in that city, and it was internally flawless, and it had one crack in it that ran all the way down.

She waited until the end, because she had learned that much.

"It only works if the thing stays what it is," she said.

The room looked at her.

"The entire property you are relying on is that nobody can make more of it and nobody can decide anything about it. That is the only reason it can hold this much without breaking. And your framework requires that the majority of it sits in eleven custodians who can be legislated, and that the layer ordinary people touch is paper written against it."

"Those aren't in tension."

"They are the same tension. They have always been the same tension." She kept her voice completely flat. "Once the paper is bigger than the metal, it is not the metal any more. It is a promise about the metal. And a promise can be extended, and it will be extended, because it will be an emergency, and it always is." She let that sit for one beat. "You will have twenty years and then you will have this same meeting, and there will be nowhere left to put it, because you will have spent the one thing that could not be spent."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That's correct," said the presenter.

Natalya blinked.

"I'm sorry?"

"You're correct. It's in the annex, it's the principal risk, we modelled it three ways and it's the same in all three." He said it without any defensiveness at all, which was the most frightening thing that happened in the room. "The framework fails in approximately twenty-two years."

"Then—"

"Ms. Freeman." He was not being cruel. He genuinely wanted her to have it. "The alternative fails in eighteen months."

---

She walked for an hour afterward, which she had not done in that city since 2016.

Nobody in the room had been lying. That was the thing she kept arriving at, on Constitution, in June, in shoes that were wrong for it. There had been no cabal and no cackling and nobody had said *and then we shall own everything*; there had been nine tired people with a genuinely impossible problem, choosing the option that put the disaster past their own retirement, exactly as every group of nine tired people has done since the invention of the calendar.

They were not going to capture it out of greed.

They were going to capture it out of *relief*.

And every institution downstream would act in its own narrow defensible interest, and the custodians would custody because that is what custodians do, and the advisers would advise the easier product because it is easier, and the households would buy the paper because the paper is what their broker offers, and not one person anywhere in the chain would ever have to decide anything wicked.

She stopped at a crossing and got her phone out and did not call anyone.

*The information is not where the decision is made.*

She had that letter. It was in a file on a laptop she had replaced twice, carried forward each time, and she had not opened it since 2019.

She stood on Constitution Avenue in June and understood, ten years late, that the machine had not been describing a fixable problem. It had been describing the load-bearing wall.
