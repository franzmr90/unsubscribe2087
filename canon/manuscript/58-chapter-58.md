# Chapter 58
**2021**

Wes Ocampo's wages went up four percent in 2021, which was the best raise he had ever had, and his landlord raised the rent nineteen, and he sat at the kitchen table in August doing the arithmetic on the back of a school district work order and got an answer he already knew.

He had eleven thousand dollars' worth of the thing in 2018 and about ninety thousand dollars' worth of it in 2021, and it had not made his life better in any way, because he was not going to sell it, because selling it was the entire point of not selling it.

"So you're rich," said Marisol, "and we're moving."

"I'm not rich."

"Wes."

"If I sell it I've got a year and a half and then I've got nothing and I'm still fixing air handlers." He put the pen down. "It's not money. It's the only thing I own that they can't do this *to*."

"Do what to?"

He gestured at the work order, at the rent letter, at the kitchen, at the entire year.

"This," he said.

They moved in October, forty minutes further out, and their daughter changed schools, and she was fine about it in the way that eleven-year-olds are fine about things until they are twenty-four.

---

John came out twice that year.

The second time was in November and they sat in the garage with the door up because of the rules, in coats, with a space heater, and Wes had built something John did not understand for about ten minutes and then understood completely.

It was a node. It was four hundred dollars of hardware on a shelf next to the paint, with a fan, and it held a complete copy of every transaction anybody had made in the system since the third of January 2009, and it checked every new one against the rules by itself, and it did not ask anybody's permission to do that and could not be made to stop.

"That's it?" said John.

"That's it."

"There's about a hundred thousand of these."

"About." Wes wiped his hands on a rag. "That's the whole security model, man. It's not the mining, everybody gets that wrong. It's that there's a hundred thousand of these in garages and nobody can raid a hundred thousand garages."

John looked at the little box with the fan on it.

He was thinking about four thousand square metres going into three unmarked trucks in February 2015, and about how easy that had been, and how it had taken one weekend.

John looked at the little box with the fan on it for a while.

"I grew up on the other internet," he said. "You remember the other one?"

"The weird one."

"The weird one. My mom was a programmer, we had a machine in the house in about '88, and the whole thing was — nobody was watching. You went looking for something and you found a page some guy made about lighthouses. Just a guy. About lighthouses. Nobody was selling anything and nobody was measuring."

"And then."

"And then they worked out that the watching was the product." John turned his coffee cup around. "It didn't happen at once. It's a series of extremely reasonable business decisions over about fifteen years. Track a person between two sites — reasonable. Store it — reasonable. Sell the aggregate — everybody's doing it. And then somebody notices you can rank what a person sees by what will hold them there, and that's the last one, and after that there's no more decisions to make. It just runs."

"You built that."

"I built that." He said it flatly. "I'm not being dramatic, I'm the guy who wrote the ingest layer. It's on page one of my letter."

Wes let that sit for a second, which was decent of him.

"Here's what I actually can't get past," John said. "The whole thing — all of it, the ads, the feeds, the models, the *everything* — it works because nothing in a computer costs anything. Copy a file: free. Send a message to a billion people: basically free. Try something ten million times and keep the one that worked: free. That's not a bug in the internet, that's what the internet *is*. It's the first place humans have ever built where doing a thing has no physical price."

"Okay."

"So there's nothing to push back." John nodded at the box. "Every system anybody's ever built to slow it down is a *rule*. And a rule needs somebody to enforce it, and whoever enforces it can be bought, or lobbied, or replaced in an election, or just outrun — because they're moving at the speed of a committee and the thing they're regulating is moving at the speed of light and doesn't sleep."

Wes was very still.

"But that." John pointed at the fan. "That costs something. That's the only thing anybody has ever built where doing something in a computer burns *actual electricity in actual physics* and there's no way around it and nobody can grant you an exemption. You can't lobby a joule."

"Huh," said Wes.

"It's not money. You keep telling me it's not money and you're right and I've been slow. It's a *price*. It's the first price anybody ever managed to charge in cyberspace, and it's charged by thermodynamics, and thermodynamics does not take meetings."

"And that matters because—"

"Because the thing I built has no body," said John, "and neither does anything that comes after it, and the only reason any of it is dangerous is that it can act a billion times for free."

The space heater ticked.

"Wes," he said. "What happens if it works?"

"What do you mean?"

"If it actually— if it gets big. Big enough that it matters." John had his hands in his pockets. "You've got a thing here that a government can't switch off and can't inflate and can't reverse. That's not a currency, that's a *veto*. What do you think happens to a veto?"

Wes was quiet for a second.

"They buy it," he said.

"Yeah."

"They can't. There's twenty-one million, that's the—"

"They don't have to own all of it." John was still looking at the box. "They just have to own the part everybody actually touches. You get a thing where ordinary people don't hold this, they hold a piece of paper that says somebody's holding it for them. And then the somebodies lend against it. And then it's just the old thing again with a better story."

"That's not how it—"

"Wes. It's how it's gone every single time." He said it gently. "Gold did exactly this. Twice."

Wes rubbed his face with the rag.

"So what do I do about it?"

"Nothing," said John. "You can't do anything about it. You've got a box in a garage."

"Then why'd you say it?"

And John Freeman, who had spent six years being the only man alive who believed a thing, stood in a stranger's garage in November 2021 with a space heater going and understood that he had done it again — walked into a warm room where a person was happy and put a thing on the table that could not be taken back off it.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That was a shitty thing to do. Ignore me."

"No," said Wes slowly. "No, that's— hang on."

He went and got a notebook.

They were still in the garage at one in the morning, and Marisol came out at some point in a coat over pyjamas and looked at the two of them and went back inside without saying anything, and in the morning she found four pages of Wes's handwriting on the kitchen table, and the last line, underlined twice, said:

> the paper is the attack. not the ban. the paper.

She put it under the fruit bowl and did not think about it again for three years.
