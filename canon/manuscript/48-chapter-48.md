# Chapter 48
The report came out on the ninth of June, and it ran to four hundred and eleven pages, and it was called *Findings of the Joint Committee on the Events of 29 January.*

It concluded that the incident had been a coordinated intrusion of unprecedented sophistication, carried out by a state or state-adjacent actor, exploiting a previously undisclosed weakness in the data-brokerage layer that sits underneath essentially everything. It noted that attribution was ongoing. It recommended eleven things, four of which were funded.

The report was not a lie. Every sentence in it was defensible and most of them were true. It was simply built around a shape that could be held in a human hand, and there was no shape available that fit what had actually happened, so the committee used the one they had.

Two members filed a dissent. It is nine pages long, it is in the back, and its central claim — that the material delivered to individuals was too specific, too well-composed, and too *kind* to be the output of a data exfiltration — was described in the coverage as speculative.

Nobody read the dissent.

By August the phrase in common use was *the Leak*, which was wrong in every particular. By the following January, when the anniversary pieces ran, the framing had settled into something about privacy legislation, and there were two documentaries, and one of them was very good.

The letters themselves were harder to explain away, and so, gradually, people stopped explaining them.

Almost nobody kept theirs. This turned out to be the most interesting thing about the whole affair and no researcher has ever satisfactorily accounted for it: four billion people were handed a complete and accurate account of their own lives, and within a year the overwhelming majority had deleted it. Not out of fear. Out of the ordinary housekeeping by which a person clears a device, over months, without deciding anything.

Item eleven on John's letter — the query he had written himself, in 2012, that ingested a file with his own name in it — he read four times in the first week and then never again.

He still has his. He is one of about nine million. He has never shown it to anyone.

---

Ubiquitous had been carrying John's health insurance and the COBRA notice arrived on the fourth of March. He got a job in October at a company that made scheduling software for hospitals, at sixty-one percent of what he had made before, and he was good at it, and nobody there knew.

Natalya was interviewed twice by people who did not give their names. She answered everything. They did not come back. In November she was hired by a policy shop in the same city at a salary that was not impressive and a title that was, and on her first day her new director said *we could not work out why you were answering telephones*, and Natalya said *no*, *neither could I*, and went and sat down at a desk with her name on it.

They were married in 2017. It was small. Pete Vandermeer came and made a speech that went on too long and mentioned the forty dollars twice.

They did not talk about it for eleven years. Not once, not properly — there were two attempts, one in 2016 in a car and one in 2019 after a funeral, and both of them ended the same way, which was that one of them would begin and the other one would let them.

The arrangement held. It was not a good arrangement. It was the one they had.

---

There is one more thing from that year and it belongs here rather than later.

On the twenty-second of April, John was in the parking deck of the hospital-software company at 6:40 in the evening, and his phone buzzed in his hand with a notification from an application he had never installed, and it said:

> 423.

He stood there in the deck with his keys out.

Then he counted, from the elevator to his car, and it was four hundred and twenty-three steps, and it had been three hundred and forty-seven at the old building for three years, and there was no possible way for anything to have known that number tonight because he had never walked it and counted, not once, not in six months at that job.

He drove home with the phone face-down on the passenger seat.

He did not tell Natalya. He told himself it was a coincidence, in the voice he used at work, and he heard himself do it, and by the time he pulled into the driveway he had almost managed it.

He was wrong about the number, incidentally. He had been wrong about it since 2012.

It was four hundred and twenty-two.

Somebody had been adding one for a very long time, to see whether he would ever say anything, and he never had, and that is the entire reason he was chosen.
