# Chapter 24
**Friday, January 23rd, 2015**

John had been ready since twenty after five.

This was a problem, because Natalya had said six, and there is nothing a man can do with forty minutes except make himself worse. He checked the mirror four times. He brushed his teeth twice. At 5:41 he took off his tie, and at 5:48 he put it back on, and at 5:52 he took it off again and threw it on the bed with more force than a tie deserves.

The jacket was the real issue.

John owned five suits, three pairs of dress shoes, and a windbreaker he had gotten for free at a corporate 5k in 2012 that said UBIQUITOUS: WE GO THE DISTANCE across the back in reflective lettering. That was the entire inventory. He had never needed anything else. The walk from the parking deck was 347 steps and the car had a heater.

*Wear a jacket, you're going to need it.*

He put on the windbreaker. He looked at himself. He took off the windbreaker. He put on a suit jacket over a sweater, which made him look like a substitute teacher, and then at 5:58 he heard the bike.

You could hear the Ducati from two streets away. It didn't rumble like the school bus that moaned past his house every morning; it made a higher, meaner noise, like something with opinions. It got louder for about fifteen seconds and then stopped in his driveway, and the silence afterward was enormous.

John put the windbreaker back on and went outside.

Natalya sat with both boots on the ground and the visor up. She looked him over once, from the shoes to the reflective lettering, and did not say anything for long enough that he began composing a defense.

"Mr. Freeman."

"Ms. Meskalov."

"What is that."

"It's a jacket."

"It is a bag."

"It's a *technical* bag. It goes the distance." He turned around so she could read the back of it. "Says so right here."

She got off the bike, walked past him into his house without being invited, and came back forty seconds later with the wool overcoat from his hall closet that he had forgotten he owned, because he had bought it three winters ago for a funeral and never worn it again.

"This one."

"That's a funeral coat."

"Then you will look very handsome," said Natalya, "at your funeral."

---

He had never been on a motorcycle. He had assumed it would feel like a fast car, which is a stupid thing to assume, in the way that assuming a shark is a fast fish is stupid.

The first thing was the cold. It came through the coat in the first thirty seconds and after that it wasn't weather anymore, it was just a fact about his body. The second thing was that there was nothing around him. In a car you are in a room that is moving. On the bike he was a man being thrown at the world at fifty-five miles an hour with nothing in between, and every time she leaned he had to decide, over and over, to trust her.

He got it wrong twice and then stopped fighting it and after that it was the best thing that had ever happened to him.

They went out past the last of the strip malls, past the outlet stores and the church with the sign that made puns about salvation, and the streetlights got further apart and then quit entirely. She turned onto a two-lane road that wound up out of the valley. The trees closed over the top of it. He could smell woodsmoke from somewhere, and cold water, and once, for maybe four seconds, something floral and completely out of season that he never identified and thought about for years afterward.

Twenty-some minutes up, she slowed and turned onto a gravel fire road, and they climbed for another few minutes at walking speed with the front tire finding every rut, and then the trees opened up.

She stopped and killed the engine.

The ridge looked west over about forty miles of nothing, and past the nothing, small and low and yellow, the city they both worked in. From here it looked like something you could pick up.

Natalya took off her helmet and shook her hair out and sat down on a flat rock like she had done it a hundred times, which she had.

"I found this in September," she said. "I was going too fast on that road and I had to stop before I killed myself. So I stopped here." She shrugged. "Now it is mine."

John sat down next to her. The rock was very cold. He decided not to mention it.

"You come up here a lot?"

"When work is bad."

"So, every day."

"Almost every day," she agreed.

---

They didn't talk for a while, which John found alarming for about ninety seconds and then didn't.

Somewhere below them a dog was barking at something, a long way off, the sound arriving thin and late. The city sat there and did whatever it was doing. His phone said NO SERVICE, which he knew because he checked it, and then felt obscurely embarrassed about checking it, and put it in his coat pocket and did not take it out again.

There was a feeling that came with that. It took him a while to notice it because he had nothing to compare it to, and he never did find the word for it. It was not peace exactly. It was more like the sensation of a sound stopping — a refrigerator hum you stop hearing until it quits, and then the room is so quiet it's almost loud.

Nobody knew where he was. Nothing knew where he was.

He didn't think about it that way. He just felt lighter and assumed it was the view.

"Can I ask you something," he said, "that's none of my business."

"You may ask."

"Why are you a secretary?"

Natalya laughed once, short, and picked up a piece of gravel and turned it over.

"Everyone asks it like that. Like it is an accusation." She threw the gravel off the edge. It didn't make any sound John could hear. "'Why are you a secretary,' with the little face. Like I fell down there."

"That's not—"

"It is. It's fine." She pulled her knees up. "You are not wrong to ask."

"I reviewed your file, Natalya. Governmental Relations, Moscow State. That's not a small thing. I've hired people from schools that aren't a tenth of that and they came in demanding a title."

"Yes."

"So."

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wasn't going to answer, and then she did, and she said it to the city and not to him.

"I studied how power works. Six years. That is what it is, Governmental Relations — it is not a diplomat degree, it is a machine degree. How does a decision get made. Who is in the room. Who is not in the room and why. And you can read about that for six years and it is like reading about swimming."

She turned the next piece of gravel over in her fingers.

"So I thought: I will go and stand next to it. Very close. I will take the smallest job in the room where the decisions happen, and I will watch, because that is the only place you can actually see it." She glanced at him. "It was not an accident, John. I did it deliberately. Nobody believes that."

"I believe it."

"Mm." She didn't sound convinced. "The problem is what nobody tells you."

"Which is?"

"To be that close, you have to be invisible. That is the price. Not one meeting have I been thrown out of — because I hold the door. Beats has said things in front of me that would end him, because I am the chair." She turned her hand over. "It works. It works perfectly. I have learned more in three months about how power actually moves than in six years of school."

"That sounds like it worked."

"It did work." She stopped. "And then in November I noticed something. Somebody asked me a question at a party — what do you do — and I heard myself say, 'I'm a secretary.' Not: I am studying something. Not: I am here for a reason. Just the flat thing. Like it was true."

She looked at the gravel.

"And it was not a lie, John. That is the part. It has become true. I got so good at being invisible that I stopped being able to find the other part underneath, and now I am not sure it is still there. Four years I have been in this country. I keep thinking I am about to start." She threw the rock. "That is why I am a secretary. Because I had a very good reason, and I used it up, and now I just work there."

She turned the last piece of gravel over and didn't throw it.

"There is a thing my professor used to say. About the men who come in at fifty and take the whole thing apart — the job, the wife, the car, all of it, and everybody laughs at them." She glanced sideways. "He said a midlife crisis is not a crisis. It is the day a human *doing* works out he was supposed to be a human *being*, and finds out nobody told him, and that he is halfway."

"That's bleak."

"It is not bleak at all, it is the only good news in the whole field." She shrugged. "The bad news is what it costs to hear it. Nobody gets that for free. It arrives with a bill."

John didn't say anything for a moment, which was the smartest thing he did all week.

"You know what I did today?" he said finally. "I got an email that solved a problem I'd been stuck on for six months. And I read it and I thought: this is the best thing anybody's ever given me. And then I thought — I didn't do it."

"You didn't write it?"

"I don't know what I did." He rubbed his hands together. "That's a hell of a thing, right? To be handed the exact thing you wanted. And the whole time you're holding it you're thinking, well, now what am I for."

Natalya looked at him for a while.

"Mr. Freeman," she said, "that is the first interesting thing you have ever said."

"I've said dozens of interesting things."

"You have said none."

"The bench thing was interesting."

"The bench thing was *athletic*," said Natalya. "It was not interesting."

---

They stayed up there another hour, which John did not know, because at some point he had stopped keeping track.

That was the thing he thought about later, when he had a great deal of time to think about it. Not the cold, or the road, or what she told him. Just that for a little while on a rock above a town, a man who had counted the steps from his car to his desk every day for three years, and knew that the number was 347, and knew what it meant when the number changed —

sat still, and did not count anything, and could not afterward have told you how long it lasted.

He got the number wrong when he tried. He said an hour.

It was an hour and twenty-two minutes.
