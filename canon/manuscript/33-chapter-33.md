# Chapter 33
**Monday, January 26th, 2015**

Marc came to the building, which he had never done, and which told Beats everything before the man opened his mouth.

"You look like hell," said Beats.

"I've been up since four." The Mayor put a folder on the desk with the care of a man setting down something that might go off. "I got him. Six days. You said a week and I did it in six."

"Sit."

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Marc."

He sat.

The name was Ellis R. Wade. Fifty-one years old. Twenty-two years with the State Bureau of Investigation, the last nine in narcotics, currently a supervisory agent with a caseload that included two of the three organizations Beats cared about. Two divorces. A boat with a note on it. A son at a private university, second year, the tuition of which exceeded the man's take-home by a margin that a person did not need an accountant to find interesting.

It was, in every respect, the file of a man with a price.

"Where did you get this?"

"Does it matter?"

"It always matters."

"A friend at the state level. He owed me from the redistricting." Marc rubbed his face. "Beats, I want to say — I did what you asked. Whatever else happens here, I did the thing."

"Mm." Beats read it again, slowly, and then a third time, and the third time he began to be unhappy without being able to say why. It was too good. Not the man — men like that are ordinary — but the *file*. There was no friction in it. Every document he wanted was there and none of the documents he did not want were there, and in twenty-six years of buying people Beats had never once been handed a portrait of a human being with no smudges on it.

He picked up the phone.

"Halloran."

"Twice in a week. I'm flattered."

"Ellis R. Wade. SBI narcotics, supervisory. What do you know."

There was a pause on the line that lasted about four seconds, and Beats, who had spent his life listening to pauses on telephones, knew before it ended that something had gone badly wrong.

"Say the name again."

"Ellis Wade. W-A-D-E."

"Beatus." Halloran's voice was very careful now. "I have been working alongside that division for eleven years. I have been to their Christmas thing. I could tell you the name of every supervisory agent in narcotics in this state and what their wives do."

"And?"

"There's no such person."

Beats looked at the folder.

"He's in the system," he said. "It's a state file. Personnel number, badge, service record, twenty-two years."

"I don't doubt it."

"You don't *doubt* it—"

"I said I don't doubt he's in the system." Halloran let that sit. "I'm telling you that I have shaken the hand of every man who has held that job since 2004, and none of them was called Wade, and I've never heard the name in my life. Both of those things are true at the same time. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

Beats did not answer.

"I'll call you back," said Halloran, and this time there was no friendliness in it at all, and he hung up first again.

Marc was watching him from the chair with the expression of a man who has just realized he is not in the room he thought he was in.

"Is there a problem?"

Beats turned a page. Ellis R. Wade looked out of a photograph at him — a heavy, tired, entirely believable face, the face of a man who has been photographed at a desk in a state building four hundred times.

Somewhere in Beats' chest something turned over that had not turned over since he was ten years old and had understood, looking at a cake with ten candles on it, that there were not going to be any presents.

"Get out," he said.

"Beats—"

"*Get out of my office.*"
